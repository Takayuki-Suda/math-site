Advanced Topics in the Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves

This final chapter is a map of that landscape as seen from the summit, and an invitation to the next climb.

Section 01

Taking stock of where we've reached ─ and a confession

A one-line look back at what we gained in Parts 1 to 5.

  • E(K) is finitely generated (weak MW + heights and descent)AEC VIII
  • The ineffectivity of that proof is concentrated at one point: determining E(K)/mE(K)AEC VIII.1.8
  • The devices that pin that point from both sides are the Selmer group (computable) and Ш (failure of the Hasse principle)AEC X §4
  • We actually computed E(ℚ) ≅ ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ all the way for one curveAEC X.4.10

That's an impressive place to have reached. But to confess honestly, along the way through these five parts we wrote a number of IOUs. The places where a green "⏩ Don't stop here" box declared "accept it for now and move on", the theorems we passed with "knowing the name is enough", the questions we closed just by writing "conjecture" ── all of them remain unpaid.

Silverman himself wrote in the preface to the first edition of AEC that "many important topics had to be omitted", and previewed those topics in an appendix at the end. The book written to make good on that preview is the sequel, "Advanced Topics in the Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves" (GTM 151, 1994). In other words, this book isn't "another, harder book" ── it's the window where our IOUs get repaid.

Gate 1 ─ ATAEC Ch. IV

Néron models ─ the proof certificate for the theorem we said "knowing the name is enough" about

✎ The IOU being repaid

In Part 1 we wrote: "[E(K) : E₀(K)] is finite (Kodaira–Néron. Knowing the name is enough)". And this fact is actually used in the proof of the Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich criterionAEC VII.7.1. In other words, the theoretical edifice of this series is built on a theorem we haven't proved.

ATAEC IV repays this debt head-on. And it repays it in grand style. Over the ring of integers R of a local field, it builds "the best integral model" of an elliptic curve E/K ── the Néron model ── and draws everything out of its universal property at once.

E(K) ≅ 𝓔(R) (Néron's universal mapping property: rational points always extend to points of the integral model)
𝓔 = the smooth part of the minimal regular model. ATAEC IV §5–§6

The possible shapes of the special fiber (what it reduces to) are completely listed by the Kodaira–Néron classification: In (n-gon), II (cusp), III, IV, In*, IV*, III*, II* ATAEC IV §8. And Silverman states plainly in ATAEC IV.9.2.1 ── "this is exactly the result that was left without proof in AEC". You can see the scene of the IOU being collected, in the original text.

Tate's algorithm ─ the classification becomes a "procedure"

A classification theorem alone is something to admire, but Tate's algorithm in ATAEC IV §9 is something to use. Feed in a Weierstrass equation, and with finitely many congruence checks the reduction type, a minimal equation, the conductor exponent f and the Tamagawa number cv = [E(Kv) : E₀(Kv)] come out mechanically. When we said in Part 5 "check the local conditions at each prime of S", this is the microscope for seeing what happens inside the bad primes.

Ogg's formula ATAEC IV.11.1

For an elliptic curve E/K over a local field K, between the minimal discriminant, the conductor and the number m(E/K) of irreducible components of the special fiber,

vK(𝔇E/K) = f(E/K) + m(E/K) − 1

holds.

Discriminant (an analytic quantity) = conductor (the amount of ramification of the Galois representation) + number of components (a geometric quantity) − 1. Quantities from three worlds balance in a single equation ── this is the completed form of the theory of "reduction" learned in Ch. VII. And the Tamagawa numbers cv appearing in the refined formula of the BSD conjectureAEC C.16.5 can't even be defined without the tools of this chapter.

? A first serious encounter with scheme theory

ATAEC IV is the most technical chapter in the book and uses the language of schemes and group schemes (the preface warns of this too). But the destination is clear: "prove everything we accepted in AEC Ch. VII". Because the motivation is so concrete, it's the best possible footing for learning abstract general theory.

Gate 2 ─ ATAEC Ch. V

The Tate curve ─ the revival of Hilbert 90

✎ The IOU being repaid

Recall the comparison table in Part 3. For the multiplicative group the theory closes with H¹(G, K×) = 0 (Hilbert 90), while for elliptic curves H¹(G, E) ≠ 0 remains, giving birth to Ш. About that "≠ 0" group WC(E/Kv), in Part 5 all we could say was "each element can be decided by Hensel's lemma". What it is as a group was left as homework.

The star of ATAEC V is an astonishing discovery by Tate. Over ℂ there was the uniformization E(ℂ) ≅ ℂ/Λ (Part 1, Ch. VI section). Rewritten with the exponential function, E(ℂ) ≅ ℂ×/qℤ (q = e2πiτ). Tate showed that this multiplicative form of uniformization holds just as well over p-adic fields.

Theorem (the Tate curve) ATAEC V.3.1 / V.5.3

Let K be a p-adic field, q ∈ K×, |q| < 1. There is an elliptic curve Eq (the Tate curve) determined by q-expansion coefficients, and

K×/qℤ ≅ Eq(K) (an analytic isomorphism compatible with the Galois action)
Δ(Eq) = q∏(1−qⁿ)²⁴, j(Eq) = 1/q + 744 + 196884q + ⋯

Conversely, any E/K with non-integral j-invariant (|j(E)| > 1) is isomorphic over K to some Eq (when the reduction is split multiplicative), and otherwise isomorphic over an extension of degree at most 2.

What matters is the phrase in bold. Uniformization over ℂ isn't compatible with Galois (you can't apply σ term by term to a convergent series), but p-adic uniformization isATAEC V.3.1.1. So it can be used for number theory. The structure of E as a Galois module splits into the multiplicative group K× and the "lattice" qℤ. Then:

Computing H¹(Gv, E) ⟶ Hilbert 90 + computing H²(Gv, qℤ) (local class field theory)

── The obstruction group that "didn't vanish" for elliptic curves, when passed through the Tate curve, turns into an exercise in Hilbert 90 and local class field theory. The moment the weapon lost in Part 3 comes back to life in the p-adic world. The consequence:

Local duality (Tate) ATAEC V Exercise 5.14
WC(E/Kv) ≅ Hom( E(Kv), ℚ/ℤ )

The local Weil–Châtelet group is nothing but the dual of the group of rational points (the exercise does the non-integral j case; the general case is also due to Tate. Over the reals, WC(E/ℝ) is 0 or ℤ/2ℤ).

Remember how in Part 5 we grinded through C17(ℚ17) = ∅ with congruences. Behind that computation stood a duality this neat. The reason the Cassels pairingAEC X.4.14 exists on Ш also traces back to this local duality. A chapter that lets you look at Part 5's landscape again from one level higher.

Gate 3 ─ ATAEC Ch. VI

Local heights ─ computing ĥ "for real"

✎ The IOU being repaid

In Part 4 §9 we defined the canonical height ĥ as lim 4⁻ⁿhx(2ⁿP), and ran a numerical experiment with E : y² = x³ + 3, P = (1,2), "reading off lim ≈ 0.9". But a definition by limit only gives slowly converging approximations. How to compute ĥ exactly by a finite procedure was left as homework.

ATAEC VI's answer is "break the global height into local heights, prime by prime".

ĥ(P) = Σv nv λ̂v(P) (local decomposition. ATAEC VI §2)
λ̂v = the canonical local height at each prime v. Existence in VI §1, explicit formulas in VI §3–§4

And each λ̂v has a closed explicit formula. At finite primes of good reduction it's the elementary formula λ̂v(P) = ½·max{ v(x(P)⁻¹), 0 }·log qv. For multiplicative reduction (type In), a formula written in the Tate curve's parameter in which the second Bernoulli polynomial B₂(t) = t² − t + 1/6 appearsATAEC VI §4. At archimedean places, a rapidly converging series using the σ function and q-expansionsATAEC VI §3. In other words, the p-adic uniformization opened by Door 2 (the Tate curve) turns into a formula for computing heights here. A place where you can hear the chapters click together.

  • Searching for generators becomes practical: "listing the points below the floor" in the descent theorem (Part 4) needed an explicit bound on |ĥ − hx|. The local decomposition reduces it to finite computations prime by prime. The height computations in today's computer algebra systems (PARI, Sage, Magma) run on exactly the formulas of this chapter.
  • You can experiment with BSD: compute the regulatorAEC VIII.9.7 numerically and match it against the L-function side ── numerical verification of the BSD conjecture first becomes possible with the techniques of this chapter.
⏩ This chapter is the "reward" slot

ATAEC VI is the most self-contained chapter in the book, and can be grafted right after AEC VIII §9. If you enjoyed Part 4, it's fine to skip the other chapters of ATAEC and start reading here.

Gate 4 ─ ATAEC Ch. III

Elliptic surfaces ─ the hunting ground for rank

✎ The IOU being repaid

At the end of Part 4 we closed with the conjecture "Is the rank unbounded?AEC VIII.10.1" left standing. So how do you build curves of large rank? Is there a systematic way to hunt, rather than searching coefficients blindly?

There is. You raise the stage by one level. A curve E/ℚ(t) whose coefficients are not numbers but functions of a variable t ── geometrically this is a surface (an elliptic surface), and ATAEC III replays all of Mordell–Weil theory on it. The weak MW theorem, heights, descent… a mountain of the same shape as the one we climbed in Parts 2–4 stands again in the world of function fields ATAEC III §2–§6. Since you've climbed it once, the second climb is faster.

And what connects the two worlds is specialization. Substituting rational numbers for t, a family of curves over ℚ is born from the curve over ℚ(t). Then:

Specialization theorem (Silverman) ATAEC III.11.4

For a non-split elliptic surface 𝓔 → C, the specialization homomorphism σt : E(K) → 𝓔t(k) is injective for all t of sufficiently large height. In particular it is injective for all but finitely many t.

Behind it is Tate's theoremATAEC III.11.1 "t ↦ ĥ𝓔ₜ(Pt) is a height function on the base curve + O(1)", and the theory of heights learned in Part 4 becomes the engine of the proof as is. The consequence is dramatic:

Build one curve of rank r over ℚ(t) ⟹ get (almost) infinitely many curves of rank ≥ r over ℚ

Néron used this method to show "there are infinitely many E/ℚ of rank ≥ 10"ATAEC III.11.4.2, Mestre built surfaces with E(ℚ(t)) of rank ≥ 12, Nagao ≥ 13. Individual curves of rank 19, 20, 21 were found one after another by specialization ── that was the scoreboard in 1994 when ATAEC was written, and Silverman closes the report with "And the quest continues!".

And indeed the quest continued. The current record is rank ≥ 29 by Elkies–Klagsbrun (2024). Meanwhile a probabilistic heuristic suggesting "the rank is actually bounded (around 21)" (Park–Poonen–Voight–Wood, 2019) has also appeared, and it's become an ongoing battlefield where experts disagree. The "quest" of a 30-year-old textbook is continuing right now ── this chapter is the entrance to that frontier.

? Where it meets geometry

The second half of ATAEC III (§7–§10) introduces intersection theory on algebraic surfaces, and links the height pairing on E(K) to the intersection form on the Néron–Severi group of the surface (the entrance to Shioda–Tate theoryATAEC III.9.7). The equation "arithmetic height = geometric intersection number" is also the entrance to the great highway leading to Arakelov geometry and Faltings' work.

Gate 5 ─ ATAEC Ch. II

Complex multiplication ─ the secret of y² = x³ − x

✎ The IOU being repaid

The signature curve of this series, E : y² = x³ − x. When we computed L = ℚ([2]⁻¹E(ℚ)) in Part 2, a "strangely beautiful field", the cyclotomic field ℚ(ζ₈), came out, and we acted surprised. That was no coincidence. It was a magic trick with a secret behind it.

The secret: E : y² = x³ − x has the endomorphism (x, y) ↦ (−x, iy), so End(E) = ℤ[i] ⊋ ℤ. This is complex multiplication (CM). ATAEC II shows that the arithmetic of curves with CM fuses completely with class field theory.

The main theorems of CM theory ATAEC II.4.3 / II.5.6–5.7 / II.6.1

Let K be an imaginary quadratic field and E an elliptic curve with End(E) ≅ RK (the ring of integers). Then:

K( j(E) ) = the Hilbert class field of K (the maximal unramified abelian extension)
K( j(E), h(Etors) ) = the maximal abelian extension of K (h is a Weber function)
j(E) is an algebraic integer

This is the imaginary quadratic version of the Kronecker–Weber theorem, "roots of unity generate the maximal abelian extension of ℚ". The role played by torsion points of ℂ× in cyclotomic theory is taken over by torsion points of elliptic curves ── the realization of what Kronecker called his "youthful dream". In Part 2, adjoining the coordinates of E[2] produced the cyclotomic field ℚ(ζ₈) because E has CM by ℤ[i] and its torsion points generate abelian extensions of ℚ(i).

What CM gives to BSD ─ analytic continuation of the L-function

There's an even deeper gift. Since the Galois representation of a CM curve is abelian, a Grössencharacter (Hecke character) ψE/L is attached to E ATAEC II §9, and by Deuring's theoremATAEC II.10.5

L(E/L, s) = L(s, ψ) · L(s, ψ̄) (a product of Hecke L-functions)

it factors. The analytic continuation and functional equation of Hecke L-functions were proved classicallyATAEC II.10.3, so for CM curves the analytic continuation of L(E,s) becomes a theorem. This is the first territory where the BSD conjecture, raised at the exit of Part 5, at least "makes complete sense as a statement". In fact, history moved from here: Coates–Wiles (1977) achieved the first major partial result on BSD, and Rubin (1987) gave the first proof of the finiteness of Ш (the moment the conjecture in X.4.13 became a theorem!) ── both for CM curves.

⏩ The only prerequisite is class field theory, and a review is included

ATAEC II uses class field theory, but a summary of the facts you need is laid out in full in II §3. For anyone who "knows the statements of class field theory but has no experience using them", CM theory is the best possible practice ground.

Gate 6 ─ ATAEC Ch. I

Modular forms ─ does L(E,s) even "exist"?

✎ The biggest IOU

At the end of Part 5 we raised the BSD conjecture ── "ords=1 L(E,s) = rank E(ℚ)". But the Euler product of the L-function of E, by the Hasse bound |ap| ≤ 2√p (the Ch. V section of Part 1!), converges only for Re(s) > 3/2. s = 1 lies outside that. In other words, with only the tools of AEC, BSD is "a sentence that doesn't yet have a meaning". This is the biggest IOU this series left behind.

There is essentially only one known way to extend L(E,s) to s = 1: show that L(E,s) is the L-function of a modular form. ATAEC I sets up that "modular forms side". The modular group SL₂(ℤ), the modular curve X(1), the q-expansion of the j-function, the infinite product for Δ(τ) (Jacobi's formulaATAEC I §8), and Hecke operatorsATAEC I §9–§10. At the top stands Hecke's theorem.

Theorem (Hecke) ATAEC I.11.4

The L-series L(f, s) of a cusp form f of weight 2k continues analytically to the whole plane and satisfies a functional equation under s ↔ 2k − s. If f is a simultaneous eigenform of the Hecke operators, L(f,s) has an Euler product.

With a single Mellin transform, the symmetry of the q-expansion (modularity) is translated into the functional equation of the L-function ── a mechanism that produces analytic continuation this vividly is worth working through by hand at least once. For the L-function of an elliptic curve to ride on this theory, you have to extend to "modular forms of level N (the congruence subgroup Γ₀(N))" and show that E is modular ── that is, that L(E,s) = L(f,s) for some cusp form f of weight 2. The preface of ATAEC (1994) left this topic to a sequel, writing "there is no question that this is currently the hottest topic in the field".

We now know what happened right after. Wiles and Taylor–Wiles (1995) proved modularity in the semistable case and deduced Fermat's Last Theorem, and Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor (2001) completed the modularity of all elliptic curves over ℚ. Now the analytic continuation of L(E,s) is (over ℚ) a theorem. Furthermore, Gross–Zagier and Kolyvagin, standing on modularity, proved "if the analytic rank is ≤ 1, the rank equality of BSD holds and Ш is finite". What we wrote in one line in Part 5, "proved in certain cases by Kolyvagin and Rubin", is exactly this story.

? Reading a 1994 textbook in the 2020s

ATAEC is a book written "just before modularity was proved". The "hottest topic" sentence in the preface records the calm just before the curtain rose on one of the great dramas in the history of mathematics. Learn the Hecke theory of Γ(1) in this book, move on to level N with Knapp or Diamond–Shurman, and stand at the entrance to Wiles' paper ── the trail that started from AEC really does connect to the highest peaks of modern number theory.

How to walk ─ routes by goal, and the first step

The six chapters of ATAEC depend on each other only lightly (the dependency chart in the preface shows only about I → II, III ⇢ IV), and you can enter through any door. Considering the connections from this series, there are three recommended routes.

ATAEC, like AEC, is not a book to read cover to cover in order. The way of reading practiced in this series ── choose a summit, pick up only the gear you need, write explicit IOUs and move on ── works as is. This time, choose the summit yourself.

Epilogue

Closing ─ every answer you gain turns one question into three

In one line, the story of this series was "from a proof of finiteness to the boundary of computability". And Part 5 ended where Ш came into view beyond that boundary.

Waiting in the sequel is mathematics that attacks that boundary from three directions. Geometry (Néron models and elliptic surfaces give shape to reduction and rank), analysis (modular forms breathe life into L-functions, and local heights make ĥ computable), algebra (class field theory completely unlocks the arithmetic of CM curves). The three directions aren't separate specialties; they meet again at a single focus, the BSD conjecture ── the rank (Part 4), the regulator (ATAEC VI), Tamagawa numbers (ATAEC IV), Ш (Part 5), and L(E,s) (ATAEC I and II). Every character you met in these notes gathers in a single equation.

Modularity has been proved. But BSD remains open, the finiteness of Ш remains open in general, and on the boundedness of rank even opinions are divided ── in other words, the best problems in this field don't belong to anyone yet. Having truly understood Chapters VIII and X of AEC, you're now standing right in front of those questions.

And the quest continues! ── J.H. Silverman, ATAEC III.11.4.2
