Between-the-lines notes ─ Weak Mordell–Weil N2/N3

A complete proof of unramifiedness
Unramifiedness outside S, with every step justified

"L/K is unramified outside S" ── this is where the gaps between the lines are widest in STEP 2 of the main part. We construct the reduction map from primitive coordinates, prove its compatibility with the Galois action by hand, and, by way of the criterion for unramifiedness via inertia groups, dispatch the issue of composite fields in one go.

TEXT: Silverman, AEC 2nd ed. ─ VIII.1.3–1.5 (fully expanded)

Main II Weak MW N0 Reduction lemma N1 Kummer pairing N2 Unramifiedness N3 Finiteness from field theory
Section 01

The gaps this note fills

  • ① Line-by-line reasons why S is a finite set (why "the coefficients are integral except at finitely many places")
  • ② The construction itself of the reduction map P ↦ P̃ (primitive coordinates, well-definedness) ── the main part accepted its existence
  • ③ A proof of the compatibility of reduction with the Galois action, (Qσ)˜ = Q̃σ̃ ── the heart of the main part's computation, yet usually waved off as "obvious" in every book
  • ④ The precise statement of "the inertia group acts trivially ⟺ unramified" (stated explicitly as a premise, with source)
  • ⑤ A proof that K(Q)/K is finite (a prerequisite for talking about "ramification of a field extension" at all)
  • ⑥ L may be the composite of infinitely many fields ── how to handle its unramifiedness

P1–P4 from N0 and Q1–Q4 from N1 remain in use. The assumption E[m] ⊂ E(K) is used only in (a), not in (b) (stated in Section 08).

Section 02

Fixing the premises (R1–R5)

We first line up all the inputs from algebraic number theory. Sources are Neukirch (shelf numbers in the appendix Number theory arsenal) and AEC.

Premise R1 ─ places, completions, ramification ANT I §8, II §8

A finite place w of a number field M comes with a normalized valuation ordw : M× ↠ ℤ, a completion Mw, a ring of integers and a residue field kw (a finite field of characteristic pw). For a finite extension M/K and w | v, the ramification index e(w/v) is defined, and on K×, ordw = e(w/v)·ordv. "M/K is unramified at v" means e(w/v) = 1 for every w above v (separability of the residue field extension is automatic since they're finite fields). e may be computed in the completions: e(w/v) = e(Mw/Kv).

Premise R2 ─ extensions to K, decomposition groups, inertia groups ANT I §9 / II §9

An extension v̄ of v to K exists. Define the decomposition group and inertia group by

Dv̄/v = { σ ∈ GK/K : v̄ ∘ σ = v̄ }, σ ∈ D induces σ̃ on the residue field k(v̄)
Iv̄/v = { σ ∈ Dv̄/v : σ̃ = idk(v̄) }

. Relation in a tower: for K ⊂ K′ ⊂ K and w′ = v̄|K′, Dv̄/w′ = Dv̄/v ∩ GK/K′, and likewise for I (immediate from the definitions: the conditions "v̄∘σ = v̄" and "σ̃ = id" are the same; only the group being moved gets smaller).

Premise R3 ─ the criterion for unramifiedness via inertia groups ANT I §9 (inertia field)

Set Tv̄ := (the fixed field of Iv̄/v) (the inertia field). For a finite extension M/K (M ⊂ K):

M ⊂ Tv̄ ⟺ M/K is unramified at the place v̄|M (e = 1)

Moreover, every place of M above v arises as the restriction v̄′|M of a suitable extension v̄′ANT II.8.1. So "M/K is unramified at v" ⟺ "M ⊂ Tv̄′ for every extension v̄′ of v".

▸ Why use this restatement (the device that resolves gap ⑥)

Translated into the inclusion condition "is it contained in Tv̄?", unramifiedness of composite fields becomes trivial: if M₁ ⊂ T and M₂ ⊂ T then M₁M₂ ⊂ T (since it's an inclusion of fields). Subextensions are trivial too: if M′ ⊂ M ⊂ T then M′ ⊂ T. This one move is overwhelmingly stronger than fighting case by case with the multiplicativity of e. We reuse it in N3 as well.

Premise R4 ─ local reduction theory AEC VII.2.1 / VII.3.1

Let F be a completion of a number field at a finite place (a local field), with residue field k of characteristic p. When E/F is given by a Weierstrass equation with F-integral coefficients and unit discriminant (= a model with good reduction):

(i) The reduction map red : E(F) → Ẽ(k) is a group homomorphismAEC VII.2.1 (good reduction, so E₀(F) = E(F)).

(ii) If p ∤ m, the restriction of red to E(F)[m] is injectiveAEC VII.3.1(b).

Open the outline of the reason for (ii) (formal groups)

The kernel E₁(F) = ker(red) is isomorphic to the formal group Ê(𝔪)AEC VII.2.2. In the formal group, the linear coefficient m of [m](T) = mT + (higher terms) is a unit since p ∤ m, so [m] : Ê(𝔪) → Ê(𝔪) is an isomorphism by the formal inverse function theoremAEC IV.2.3. In particular Ê(𝔪) has no m-torsion. So E(F)[m] ∩ E₁(F) = {O}, i.e. restricting red to E(F)[m] has trivial kernel = injective.

Premise R5 ─ compatibility with completion

For a number field M and a finite place w, E(M) ⊂ E(Mw), and reduction defined using the valuation ring O(w) = {a ∈ M : ordw(a) ≥ 0} of M (Section 04) agrees on E(M) with reduction defined using the ring of integers of Mw. Reason: O(w) is the intersection of M with the ring of integers of Mw, and the residue fields are the sameANT II.4.3. Primitive coordinates are primitive whichever ring you view them in.

Section 03

S is a finite set (gap ①)

S = { v : E has bad reduction at v } ∪ { v : v(m) ≠ 0 } ∪ MK∞
1

Fix one Weierstrass equation: coefficients a₁, …, a₆ ∈ K, discriminant Δ ≠ 0. For each coefficient ai, { v ∈ MK0 : v(ai) < 0 } is finite.

▸ Reason The fractional ideal (a) of a ∈ K× factors uniquely into finitely many prime idealsANT I §3. v(a) ≠ 0 only for the v corresponding to those prime factors.

2

{ v : v(Δ) ≠ 0 } is finite too. { v : v(m) ≠ 0 } is finite too. MK∞ has at most [K:ℚ] elements.

▸ Reason Same as Step 1 (Δ, m ∈ K×). The number of infinite places is the number of real embeddings + pairs of complex embeddingsANT I §5.

3

At a finite place v ∉ S, the fixed equation has v-integral coefficients and v(Δ) = 0. Hence E has good reduction at v, and this equation itself is a good model.

▸ Reason If the coefficients are integral and the discriminant is a unit, the reduced equation is a Weierstrass equation over kv with discriminant Δ̃ = Δ mod 𝔪v ≠ 0, i.e. nonsingular = an elliptic curve. "A model with integral coefficients and unit discriminant exists ⟺ good reduction"AEC VII.5.1(b). (This model is also minimal at v: among integral models v(Δ) = 0 is the minimum value.)

4

Extending doesn't break it: if K′/K is finite, w | v and v ∉ S, the same equation has w-integral coefficients and w(Δ) = 0. That is, E/K′ also has good reduction at w, and the reduced curve is Ẽv with its coefficients extended.

▸ Reason For an element a of K, ordw(a) = e(w/v)·ordv(a) (R1). ordv(ai) ≥ 0 ⇒ ordw(ai) ≥ 0, and ordv(Δ) = 0 ⇒ ordw(Δ) = 0. Note that this holds even if it's ramified (e times 0 is still 0).

Section 04

Constructing the reduction map, and Galois compatibility (gaps ②③)

Lemma N2-A (constructing the reduction map)

Let M be a number field, w a finite place, O(w) = {a ∈ M : ordw(a) ≥ 0}, k the residue field. Then a map red : ℙ²(M) → ℙ²(k) is well defined as follows: take a representative of A = [x₀ : x₁ : x₂] with mini ordw(xi) = 0 (primitive coordinates), and set red(A) := [x̄₀ : x̄₁ : x̄₂]. Moreover, if E is given by an equation with w-integral coefficients and unit discriminant, red(E(M)) ⊂ Ẽ(k).

1

Primitive coordinates exist.

▸ Reason For any representative (x₀,x₁,x₂) ≠ 0, n := mini ordw(xi) ∈ ℤ. There is π ∈ M with ordw(π) = 1 (ordw is surjective: R1), so multiplying by π−n makes min = 0.

2

Primitive coordinates are unique up to multiplication by a unit. Hence red(A) doesn't depend on the choice of representative.

▸ Reason Two sets of primitive coordinates differ by a factor λ ∈ M× (definition of projective coordinates). Both have min 0, so ordw(λ) = 0, i.e. λ is a unit. The reduction of a unit is an element of k×, so after reduction it's the same point in projective coordinates. Also min = 0 means at least one x̄i ≠ 0, i.e. (x̄) ≠ (0,0,0), so red(A) really is a point of ℙ²(k).

3

If A ∈ E(M), then red(A) ∈ Ẽ(k).

▸ Reason The homogeneous equation F(x₀,x₁,x₂) = 0 of E has coefficients in O(w). Passing through the ring homomorphism O(w) → k gives 0 = the reduction of F(x) = F̃(x̄). F̃ is the defining equation of Ẽ.

Lemma N2-B (Galois compatibility) ── the core of gap ③

Suppose further that M/K is Galois, E is defined over K, and σ ∈ GM/K satisfies w ∘ σ = w. σ preserves O(w) and its maximal ideal, so it induces σ̃ : k → k on the residue field. Then for every A ∈ E(M),

red(Aσ) = red(A)σ̃
4

Taking primitive coordinates (x₀,x₁,x₂) of A, (x₀σ, x₁σ, x₂σ) are primitive coordinates of Aσ.

▸ Reason ordw(xiσ) = (ordw∘σ)(xi) = ordw(xi) (assumption w∘σ = w). So the min stays 0. Aσ ∈ E(M) because the coefficients of E are elements of K, invariant under σ (the same reasoning as P2 in N0).

5

red(Aσ) = [ (x₀σ)¯ : (x₁σ)¯ : (x₂σ)¯ ] = [ x̄₀σ̃ : x̄₁σ̃ : x̄₂σ̃ ] = red(A)σ̃.

▸ Reason The first equality is Step 4 (we may compute the reduction with primitive coordinates: uniqueness in Lemma N2-A). The second is the definition of σ̃ (σ̃(ā) := (σa)¯, well defined because σ preserves the maximal ideal).

N2-A, N2-B ∎
? What these two lemmas mean

The main part's one line "(Qσ − Q)˜ = Q̃σ − Q̃" is actually a set of three: N2-A (well-definedness) + N2-B (compatibility) + R4(i) (homomorphism). Every textbook passes through it as "obvious", but once you go down to primitive coordinates it's all just computations with valuations.

Section 05

Proof of the main theorem (b) ─ L/K is unramified outside S

Theorem AEC VIII.1.5(b)

Let L = K([m]−1E(K)). Then L/K is unramified outside S.

1

Restating the goal: it's enough to show, for any v ∉ S and any extension v̄, that "K(Q) ⊂ Tv̄ for every Q ∈ [m]−1E(K)".

▸ Reason If we can say this, L = (the composite of the K(Q)) ⊂ Tv̄. Then every finite subextension M of L also has M ⊂ Tv̄, so by R3 M is unramified at v̄|M. Since v̄ is arbitrary, it's unramified at every place above v (second half of R3), and since v ∉ S is arbitrary, it meets the definition of "unramified outside S" (= every finite subextension is unramified outside S). Gap ⑥ disappears here.

2

K(Q)/K is finite, with [K(Q) : K] ≤ m² (gap ⑤).

▸ Reason The fiber [m]−1(P) of P := [m]Q ∈ E(K) is { Q + T : T ∈ E[m] }, exactly m² points (taking differences lands in ker[m] = E[m]: P1, P4). For any τ ∈ GK/K, [m](Qτ) = Pτ = P (P2), so Qτ is an element of this fiber. A K-embedding K(Q) ↪ K extends to an automorphism τ of K (Q4), and where the generators x(Q), y(Q) go is determined by Qτ, so the number of embeddings ≤ #fiber = m². In char 0, degree = number of embeddings (separability).

3

Fix arbitrary v ∉ S, an extension v̄, σ ∈ Iv̄/v, and Q ∈ [m]−1E(K). Goal: Qσ = Q. Let M := the Galois closure of K(Q) (⊂ K); then M/K is finite Galois and contains the coordinates of Q and Qσ.

▸ Reason Existence and finiteness of the Galois closure are field theory (K(Q)/K is finite by Step 2). The coordinates of Qσ are conjugates of x(Q), y(Q), so they lie in the Galois closure.

4

Set w := v̄|M. E has good reduction at w, and the reduction map red : E(Mw) → Ẽ(kw) is a group homomorphism. On E(M) it agrees with reduction via primitive coordinates (N2-A).

▸ Reason Good reduction: Section 03 Step 4 (v ∉ S, w | v). Homomorphism: R4(i). Agreement: R5.

5

Setting R := Qσ − Q, R ∈ E(M)[m].

▸ Reason Q, Qσ ∈ E(M) (Step 3), and E(M) is a group (P1). [m]R = [m](Qσ) − [m]Q = ([m]Q)σ − [m]Q (P2) = O, the last because [m]Q ∈ E(K) is fixed by σ (P2). E[m] ⊂ E(K) is not used here.

6

σ|M ∈ GM/K satisfies w∘(σ|M) = w, and the induced σ̃ is the identity on kw.

▸ Reason σ(M) = M (M/K is Galois: normality). w∘σ|M = (v̄∘σ)|M = v̄|M = w (σ ∈ I ⊂ D, R2). The embedding of residue fields kw ⊂ k(v̄) is induced by the inclusion of valuation rings O(w) ⊂ Ov̄, and σ̃ on k(v̄) is the identity (the definition of σ ∈ Iv̄/v: R2), so its restriction σ̃ on kw is the identity too. This is a concrete form of the tower relation in R2, "the restriction of Iv̄/v lies in the lower inertia group".

7

red(R) = red(Qσ) − red(Q) = red(Q)σ̃ − red(Q) = red(Q) − red(Q) = Õ.

▸ Reason First equality: red is a homomorphism (Step 4). Second: Galois compatibility N2-B (the conditions of Step 6 hold). Third: σ̃ = id (Step 6). This is the full expansion of the main part's one-line computation.

8

The characteristic p of kw does not divide m.

▸ Reason If p | m, then v(m) ≥ v(p) ≥ 1 (v is a place above p: w | v | p), contradicting v(m) = 0 (v ∉ S).

9

R = O, i.e. Qσ = Q.

▸ Reason R ∈ E(M)[m] ⊂ E(Mw)[m] (R5). By R4(ii) (its condition p ∤ m was checked in Step 8), red is injective on E(Mw)[m]. red(R) = Õ = red(O) (Step 7), so R = O.

10

Therefore Iv̄/v fixes x(Q), y(Q), and K(Q) ⊂ Tv̄. Since Q was arbitrary, theorem (b) follows by the restatement in Step 1.

▸ Reason Step 9 holds for any σ ∈ Iv̄/v (σ was fixed arbitrarily in Step 3). Fixing a point ⇒ fixing its affine coordinates (same argument as P3 in N0). Definition of fixed field.

(b) ∎
Section 06

(a) L/K is abelian with exponent dividing m

1

Use the injective homomorphism ρ : GL/K ↪ Hom(E(K)/mE(K), E[m]) from Step 18 of N1.

▸ Reason N1 (at this stage E[m] ⊂ E(K) is assumed).

2

The right side is an abelian group under "valuewise addition", and every element f satisfies m·f = 0.

▸ Reason Because the value group E[m] is abelian of exponent m ((m·f)(x) = [m](f(x)) = O).

3

GL/K is isomorphic to a subgroup of an abelian group, so it's abelian, and every element has order dividing m (exponent | m).

▸ Reason The image of an injective homomorphism is a subgroup. Subgroups inherit commutativity and the exponent condition.

(a) ∎
Section 07

Summary, and which assumptions were used

What we know about L/K at this point
  • An abelian extension whose Galois group has exponent dividing m ((a) ── uses E[m] ⊂ E(K))
  • Unramified outside the finite set S ((b) ── does not use E[m] ⊂ E(K). This fact is reused as is in Part 5 for the finiteness of Selmer groups)
  • We haven't yet said it's finite ── saying that is the job of N3
? The precise meaning of "the local constrains the global"

Lining the proof up step by step, the global field-theoretic conclusion "unramified" reduces completely to just three equalities in Step 7 ── homomorphism, Galois compatibility, trivial action of inertia ── and the injectivity from formal groups in Step 9. R = Qσ − Q is squeezed between the information geometry makes invisible (Step 7) and the information geometry doesn't lose (Step 9), and vanishes. That's what "good reduction forbids ramification" really is.
