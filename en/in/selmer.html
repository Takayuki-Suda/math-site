The Selmer and Shafarevich–Tate Groups

Where is the Mordell–Weil theorem "computable", and where does it become "uncomputable"? Drawing that boundary precisely is the job of the Selmer group and Ш. We read AEC X §4 closely and actually compute the Mordell–Weil group of one curve all the way through. Finally we survey Ch. IX and the rest of Ch. X to close out the series.

Section 01

Resetting the problem ─ winning back the lost computability

Let's sort out the situation established so far in the series.

  • E(K) is finitely generated (Part 4). Moreover, as long as we know generators of E(K)/mE(K), generators of E(K) can be computed effectively by descentAEC VIII.3.2 / Exercise 8.18.
  • E(K)/mE(K) is finite and injects into the computable finite group H¹(GK/K, E[m]; S) (classes unramified outside S) (Parts 2 and 3).
  • But there is no method to decide "which elements of H¹ come from rational points"AEC VIII.1.8. This is the one ineffective point.

The idea of Chapter X is this: we can't decide "does it come from a rational point?", but we can decide "could it come from a rational point locally?". That's because each completion Kv is "an easy world to compute in", thanks to Hensel's lemma. So we insert in between the group obtained by cutting H¹ with local conditions ── the Selmer group ── and name the error left over Ш and study it head-on. That's the design of X §4.

Section 02 ─ Preparation (summary)

The tools of X §1–§3 ─ a dictionary called homogeneous spaces

X §1: complete 2-descent (you already know half of it)

This is about determining the image of "when E[2] ⊂ E(K), E(K)/2E(K) ↪ (K×/(K×)²)², P ↦ (x−e₁, x−e₂)", seen in Part 3 §7AEC X.1.1 / X.1.4. The key is that the image lands in pairs from the finite group

K(S, m) = { b ∈ K×/(K×)m : ordv(b) ≡ 0 (mod m) (∀v ∉ S) }
The same as TS in Part 2 STEP 3. S = {bad reduction} ∪ {v | 2} ∪ MK∞

, and that whether (b₁,b₂) ∈ K(S,2)² lies in the image can be restated as the existence of a rational point on an auxiliary curve

b₁z₁² − b₂z₂² = e₂ − e₁, b₁z₁² − b₁b₂z₃² = e₃ − e₁

AEC X.1.4. As an example AEC completely computes y² = x(x−2)(x−10) and shows its rank is 0AEC X.1.5. The form "computing E(K)/2E(K) = searching for rational points on finitely many auxiliary curves" is the prototype of this whole chapter.

X §2–§3: twists, homogeneous spaces, the Weil–Châtelet group

In Part 3 we said "H¹(G, E(K)) ≠ 0 is what makes the theory of elliptic curves hard". X §3 gives this group a geometric meaning.

Definition (homogeneous space) AEC X §3

A (principal) homogeneous space (torsor) for E/K is a pair of a smooth curve C/K of genus 1 and a simply transitive action C × E → C defined over K. The trivial example is C = E itself. Two homogeneous spaces are identified if K-isomorphic, and the set of equivalence classes is called the Weil–Châtelet group WC(E/K).

We use only the following three facts (you may skim the proofs):

  • {C/K} is trivial ⟺ C(K) ≠ ∅AEC X.3.3. "A torsor with a K-rational point can be identified with E."
  • WC(E/K) ≅ H¹(GK/K, E(K))AEC X.3.6. The correspondence chooses a K-isomorphism θ : C → E and takes a cocycle of the form σ ↦ θσ(p) − θ(p).
  • The Jacobian of C (Pic⁰(C)) gives back EAEC X.3.8. A dictionary: "a torsor of E = a genus-1 curve whose Jacobian is E".
Language of cohomologyLanguage of geometry
ξ ∈ H¹(G, E)A genus-1 curve C/K (with Jacobian E)
ξ = 0C(K) ≠ ∅ (it has a rational point)
resv(ξ) = 0 ∈ H¹(Gv, E)C(Kv) ≠ ∅ (it has a v-adic point)
⏩ Don't stop here

X §2 (the general theory of twists) and the proofs of X §3 are parts you may pass through on a first reading carrying only the dictionary above (three items + table). With the dictionary in hand you can follow every argument of §4. When you need the details of the theory of homogeneous spaces, come back to AEC X §3.

Section 03

The basic diagram and the definitions of the two groups AEC X §4

Let φ : E → E′ be an isogeny defined over K (E = E′, φ = [m] is the typical example. We generalize because we really use it in "descent via 2-isogeny" (§5)). From the short exact sequence 0 → E[φ] → E → E′ → 0, by the same procedure as Part 3, we get the global basic exact sequence:

0 → E′(K)/φ(E(K)) →δ H¹(GK/K, E[φ]) → WC(E/K)[φ] → 0 (∗)

Doing the same at each place v (Gv ⊂ GK/K the decomposition group) gives the local version:

0 → E′(Kv)/φ(E(Kv)) →δ H¹(Gv, E[φ]) → WC(E/Kv)[φ] → 0 (∗v)

And the commutative diagram tying the two together via restriction maps is the stage for every argument in this section:

Figure 1: the basic commutative diagram (∗∗) [AEC X §4]. The top row is the global (∗), the bottom row the product of the local (∗v). Goal = determine the image of the top left (rational points). The bottom right (local WC) is computable thanks to Hensel's lemma.
Definition (Selmer group, Tate–Shafarevich group) AEC X §4
S(φ)(E/K) = ker{ H¹(GK/K, E[φ]) → ∏v WC(E/Kv) } (the φ-Selmer group)
Ш(E/K) = ker{ WC(E/K) → ∏v WC(E/Kv) } (the Tate–Shafarevich group)

Neither depends on the choice of extension of v to KAEC X.4.1.1.

Read through the geometric dictionary (§2), the meaning of the definitions becomes vivid:

  • S(φ) = "cohomology classes whose corresponding homogeneous space has a point over every completion". That is, the collection of "classes that locally can't be told apart from those coming from rational points".
  • Ш = "torsors that are locally trivial everywhere, yet globally nontrivial"AEC X.4.1.2. That is, the group that measures the failure of the Hasse principle (the local–global principle). For curves of genus 0 (conics), the counterpart of this group vanishes by the Hasse–Minkowski theorem, but in genus 1 it doesn't.
Section 04

The basic theorem ─ exact sequence and finiteness

Theorem AEC X.4.2

If φ : E/K → E′/K is an isogeny, then:

(a) 0 → E′(K)/φ(E(K)) → S(φ)(E/K) → Ш(E/K)[φ] → 0 is exact
(b) S(φ)(E/K) is a finite group
Proof of (a) (writing out the whole diagram chase)

The image lands in Selmer: for P ∈ E′(K), δ(P) goes to 0 in WC(E/K) by exactness of the global sequence (∗). By commutativity of the diagram, its image at each v (∈ WC(E/Kv)) is also 0. So δ(P) ∈ S(φ).

S(φ) → Ш[φ] can be defined: let c be the image in WC(E/K)[φ] of ξ ∈ S(φ). Then by commutativity each local image of c equals the local image of ξ, which is 0. So c ∈ Ш[φ].

Surjectivity: take any c ∈ Ш(E/K)[φ]. By exactness of the global sequence (∗) (the right end is surjective), c is the image of some ξ ∈ H¹(G, E[φ]). The local images of ξ go to the local images of c = 0, so ξ ∈ S(φ).

Exactness at S(φ): ξ ∈ S(φ) goes to 0 in Ш[φ] ⟺ the image of ξ in WC(E/K) is 0 ⟺ (exactness of the global sequence) ξ ∈ im δ.

∎
Proof of (b) (a replay of the weak MW argument)

Let ξ ∈ S(φ), and let v be a finite place where "v doesn't divide deg φ = m and E has good reduction". We show ξ is unramified at v.

Since ξ ∈ S(φ), ξ is trivial in WC(E/Kv). By exactness of the local sequence (∗v), there is P ∈ E(Kv) with ξσ = Pσ − P (∀σ ∈ Gv), and moreover Pσ − P ∈ E[φ] ⊂ E[m]. Taking σ in the inertia group Iv, inertia doesn't move anything after reduction, so (Pσ − P)˜ = Õ. That is, Pσ − P is a point that is "m-torsion and in the kernel of reduction", and by VIII.1.4 (= VII.3.1, the weapon of Part 2 STEP 2) it's O. So ξσ = 0 (∀σ ∈ Iv), and ξ is unramified at v.

Therefore S(φ) ⊂ H¹(G, E[φ]; S) (S = {bad reduction} ∪ {v | deg φ} ∪ MK∞, a finite setAEC X.4.4. That E and E′ have the same bad primes [AEC VII.7.2] also comes into play here). The rest is the following lemma.

∎ (except for the lemma)
Lemma (finiteness of cohomology unramified outside S) AEC X.4.3

If M is a finite GK/K-module and S a finite set of places, then H¹(GK/K, M; S) = { ξ : unramified outside S } is finite.

Proof

M is finite, so there is an open subgroup fixing every element of M. By inflation–restriction (Part 3) we may replace K by a finite extension, and we may assume the action is trivial. Then H¹(G, M; S) = Hom(G, M; S). Let m be the exponent of M, and L/K "the maximal abelian extension of exponent m unramified outside S"; then every homomorphism G → M unramified outside S factors through GL/K. By VIII.1.6 (Part 2 STEP 3!) L/K is finite, so Hom(GL/K, M) is finite.

∎
? "The proof of weak MW" and "the finiteness of Selmer groups" are the same theorem

When φ = [m], the left end of the exact sequence in (a) is E(K)/mE(K), so the weak MW theorem follows from the finiteness in (b). Conversely, the proof of (b) is a direct replay of Part 2's STEP 2 (unramifiedness) and STEP 3 (finiteness from field theory). In other words, the "body" of the proof of the weak Mordell–Weil theorem is the finiteness of the Selmer group, and nothing learned in Part 2 has been wasted. The whole series comes together here.

Selmer groups are computable AEC X.4.5

H¹(G, E[φ]; S) is a computable finite group (by following Part 2 STEP 3 explicitly). For each element ξ of it, "is ξ ∈ S(φ)?" means deciding whether the corresponding homogeneous space Cξ has a Kv-point for each v ∈ S, which reduces to finitely many congruence checks thanks to Hensel's lemma (for v ∉ S, local points exist automatically by unramifiedness). So S(φ)(E/K) can always, in principle, be computed. What can't be computed is only Ш[φ] ── "global obstructions invisible locally".

Section 05

Descent via 2-isogeny ─ a tool for real computations AEC X.4.8–4.9

For a curve with a K-rational 2-torsion point T = (0,0), we can split [2] into "the composition of two isogenies of degree 2" and lighten the descent computation. It can always be used when E(K)[2] ≠ 0, and it's the most important form in practiceAEC X.4.9.2.

Proposition (descent via 2-isogeny) AEC X.4.9
E : y² = x³ + ax² + bx, E′ : Y² = X³ − 2aX² + (a² − 4b)X
φ : E → E′, φ(x,y) = ( y²/x², y(b − x²)/x² ), ker φ = { O, (0,0) }, deg φ = 2
S = MK∞ ∪ { v : v(2) ≠ 0 or v(b) ≠ 0 or v(a²−4b) ≠ 0 }

For d ∈ K×, define the homogeneous space Cd by

Cd : dw² = d² − 2adz² + (a² − 4b)z⁴

; then there is an exact sequence

0 → E′(K)/φ(E(K)) →δ K(S,2) → WC(E/K)[φ]
δ(O) = 1, δ((0,0)) = a² − 4b, δ((X,Y)) = X (X ≠ 0) / d ↦ {Cd/K}

, and the Selmer group is

S(φ)(E/K) ≅ { d ∈ K(S,2) : Cd(Kv) ≠ ∅ (∀v ∈ S) }

Moreover ψ : Cd → E′, ψ(z,w) = ( d/z², −dw/z³ ) satisfies δ(ψ(P)) ≡ d when a point P ∈ Cd(K) is found (a device for recovering pointsAEC X.4.6).

E′ has the same form as E, so the same proposition can be used for the dual isogeny φ̂ : E′ → E. The results of the two descents combine into E(K)/2E(K) via the following exact sequenceAEC X.4.7:

0 → E′(K)[φ̂] / φ(E(K)[2]) → E′(K)/φ(E(K)) →φ̂ E(K)/2E(K) → E(K)/φ̂(E′(K)) → 0
(obtained by an elementary diagram chase using φ̂∘φ = [2])
Section 06 ─ A complete worked example

Real computation: the Mordell–Weil group of E : y² = x³ − 6x² + 17x AEC X.4.10

a = −6, b = 17. The discriminant is Δ = −2⁹·17², so S = {∞, 2, 17}, ℚ(S,2) = {±1, ±2, ±17, ±34} (8 elements). The partner under the 2-isogeny is

E′ : Y² = X³ + 12X² − 32X, Cd : dw² = d² + 12dz² − 32z⁴

Determining the Selmer group on the φ side

  • d = −2 ∈ S(φ): the image of (0,0) ∈ E′(ℚ) is δ(0,0) = a²−4b = −32 ≡ −2. It comes from a rational point, so it automatically lies in Selmer.
  • d = 2 ∈ S(φ) (and it comes from a rational point): rewriting C₂ : 2w² = 4 + 24z² − 32z⁴ with z = Z/2 gives w² = 2 + 3Z² − Z⁴. By inspection, a rational point (Z, w) = (1, 2), i.e. (z,w) = (1/2, 2) ∈ C₂(ℚ). With the recovery device ψ, ψ(1/2, 2) = (8, −32) ∈ E′(ℚ); check: δ(8,−32) = 8 ≡ 2. ✓
  • d = 17 ∉ S(φ) (it dies 17-adically): suppose C₁₇ : 17w² = 17² + 12·17z² − 32z⁴ has a ℚ₁₇-point. Comparing valuations, noting that ord₁₇(17w²) is odd and ord₁₇(32z⁴) is even, we need z, w ∈ ℤ₁₇. Then from the equation, in turn, z ≡ 0, w ≡ 0 (mod 17), and finally 17² ≡ 0 (mod 17³), a contradiction. So C₁₇(ℚ₁₇) = ∅.
  • The rest is handled automatically by the group structure: S(φ) is a subgroup of ℚ(S,2), containing {1, 2, −2, −1} and not containing 17. If any of ±17, ±34 were in it, 17 would be too (multiplying by 2, −1). So S(φ)(E/ℚ) = {±1, ±2} ≅ (ℤ/2)².

Moreover, since we found that all four elements of S(φ) come from points of E′(ℚ) (O, (0,0), (8,−32), and their sum), the exact sequence (X.4.2a) gives Ш(E/ℚ)[φ] = 0. This is a lucky case.

The φ̂ side (from E′ to E)

  • C′d : dw² = d² − 24dz² + 272z⁴ (a′ = 12, b′ = −32, a′²−4b′ = 272).
  • The image of (0,0) ∈ E(ℚ): δ(0,0) = 272 ≡ 17. So 17 ∈ S(φ̂).
  • d < 0 dies over the reals: if d < 0 the left side dw² ≤ 0, while the right side = d² − 24dz² + 272z⁴ has every term ≥ 0 and d² > 0, so it's positive. C′d(ℝ) = ∅.
  • d = 2 dies 2-adically: rewriting C′₂ with z = Z/2 gives 2w² = 4 − 12Z² + 17Z⁴. If there were a ℚ₂-point we'd need Z, w ∈ ℤ₂, and mod 2 in turn Z ≡ 0, w ≡ 0, and finally 4 ≡ 0 (mod 8), a contradiction.
  • In summary, S(φ̂)(E′/ℚ) = {1, 17} ≅ ℤ/2, and both come from rational points, so Ш(E′/ℚ)[φ̂] = 0.

Assembly

E′(ℚ)/φ(E(ℚ)) ≅ (ℤ/2)² (generated by (0,0), (8,−32))
E(ℚ)/φ̂(E′(ℚ)) ≅ ℤ/2 (generated by (0,0))
Substituting into the exact sequence of X.4.7 ⇒ E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/2)²

E(ℚ)[2] = {O, (0,0)} (x² − 6x + 17 has negative discriminant and no rational root), so from dim₂ E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) = rank + dim₂ E(ℚ)[2], rank = 1. Conclusion:

E(ℚ) ≅ ℤ × ℤ/2ℤ
An example of a point of infinite order: (4, 6) ∈ E(ℚ) (64 − 96 + 68 = 36 = 6²)

This is the entire process of "computing a Mordell–Weil group". The finiteness guaranteed by weak MW (Part 2) is actually determined with the computable container called the Selmer group, and the descent theorem (Part 4) tells us "all that's left is to search for generators". Every part of the theory is in motion.

Section 07

Is Ш really nontrivial? ─ failure of the Hasse principle AEC X.4.11

All the examples so far were "lucky", with Ш[φ] = 0. But Ш doesn't vanish in general. The first examples in history are due to Lind (1940) and Reichardt (1942):

C : 2w² = 1 − 17z⁴
has points over every ℚp and ℝ, but no ℚ-points (proved in Exercise 5)

This C is a homogeneous space of a suitable elliptic curve, and gives a nontrivial element (of order 2) of Ш. A little later Selmer (1951) showed that the cubic curve

3x³ + 4y³ + 5z³ = 0

is an example of the same kind (an element of Ш of order 3 for the Jacobian of x³ + y³ + 60z³ = 0), and studied large families. The name "Selmer group" comes from this work.

How far can we attack Ш? AEC X.4.12–4.14

  • Refined descentAEC X.4.12: using the image S(m,n) ⊂ S(m) of S(mⁿ), 0 → E(K)/mE(K) → S(m,n) → mn−1Ш[mⁿ] → 0. Raising n while running a search for rational points in parallel, the rank will eventually be determined unless Ш has "elements infinitely divisible by m".
  • The Ш finiteness conjectureAEC X.4.13: the conjecture that Ш(E/K) is finite. Proved in certain cases (analytic rank ≤ 1, etc.) by Kolyvagin and Rubin. If finiteness is known, the procedure above always stops = computing Mordell–Weil groups becomes a complete algorithm.
  • The Cassels pairingAEC X.4.14: there is an alternating bilinear pairing Γ : Ш × Ш → ℚ/ℤ whose kernel consists only of divisible elements. In particular, if Ш is finite, its order is a perfect square. An astonishing theorem: "such a beautiful constraint can be proved about the order of a group not even known to be finite".
? A word about the BSD conjecture (the series' exit)

The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjectureAEC C.16.5 connects the rank r and Ш to the behavior of the L-function L(E,s) at s = 1: ords=1 L(E,s) = r, and #Ш and the regulator (Part 4 §9) appear in the formula for the leading coefficient. The landscape of modern number theory is that all the objects learned in this series ── rank, ĥ, Ш ── gather into a single analytic formula.

Section 08 ─ Survey (summary)

Ch. IX integral points / the rest of Ch. X

Ch. IX: integral points (Siegel's theorem)

If Mordell–Weil is the structure theorem for "rational points", Ch. IX is the finiteness theorem for "integral points". The main result is Siegel's theorem: a Weierstrass equation has finitely many S-integral pointsAEC IX §3–§4. The proof uses Diophantine approximation (Roth's theoremAEC IX §1, §8) and, remarkably, the Mordell–Weil theorem itself (divide points by m to improve the quality of approximation, a relative of descent). The effective version via Baker's methodAEC IX §5 and Shafarevich's theorem "there are finitely many elliptic curves with good reduction outside S"AEC IX.6.1 are also in this chapter.

⏩ Knowing where it fits is enough

Ch. IX is an application of VIII and can be read independently of X §4. For the purpose of this series (understanding VIII), understanding the single sentence "Mordell–Weil combines with approximation theorems to give finiteness of integral points" is enough.

Ch. X §5–§6: twists in concrete terms

§5 is the general theory of twists as elliptic curves (quadratic twists E(d) : dy² = x³ + Ax + B, etc.) AEC X.5.4, and §6 is a large-scale worked example using them to analyze the Mordell–Weil groups of the family Y² = X³ + DX for each DAEC X §6. If you understand up to §4, §6 is the best possible material as "a practice ground to test your skills".

Problem 1★ Basic

Reconstruct, without looking at the text, the diagram chase deriving the exact sequence 0 → E′(K)/φ(E(K)) → S(φ) → Ш[φ] → 0 (X.4.2a) from the basic diagram (∗∗). In particular, make explicit where the surjectivity at the right end of the global sequence (∗) is used in the "surjectivity" argument.

Open the solution

Reconstruct the four paragraphs of Section 04 in the text (the image lands / the map can be defined / surjectivity / exactness in the middle). Surjectivity: for c ∈ Ш[φ] ⊂ WC(E/K)[φ], take a lift ξ using that H¹(G,E[φ]) → WC(E/K)[φ] in (∗) is surjective. This surjectivity holds because in the long exact sequence the term after WC[φ] is the part going to φ times H¹(G,E) itself (cutting out a Kummer-type exact sequence). The local images of the lift are 0 because c ∈ Ш.

Problem 2★ Basic

Write out the argument for C₁₇(ℚ₁₇) = ∅ in Section 06 of the text (parity of 17-adic valuations), filling in every gap. That is: assuming C₁₇ : 17w² = 17² + 12·17z² − 32z⁴ has (z,w) ∈ ℚ₁₇², show in order (i) z, w ∈ ℤ₁₇ are necessary, (ii) z ≡ 0 (mod 17), (iii) w ≡ 0 (mod 17), (iv) a contradiction.

Open the solution

(i) If ord(z) < 0, the valuation of the right side is 4·ord(z) (the −32z⁴ term dominates; ord₁₇(32)=0), which is even, while the left side has 1 + 2·ord(w), which is odd, a contradiction. So z ∈ ℤ₁₇. Then the valuation of the right side is clearly ≥ 0, and from the left side 1 + 2ord(w) ≥ 0 we get ord(w) ≥ 0 (valuations are integers), w ∈ ℤ₁₇. (ii) Mod 17, 0 ≡ −32z⁴, and 32 is a unit, so z ≡ 0 (mod 17). (iii) Substitute z = 17z₁: 17w² = 17² + 12·17³z₁² − 32·17⁴z₁⁴. Dividing by 17, w² = 17 + 12·17²z₁² − 32·17³z₁⁴ ≡ 0 (mod 17), so w ≡ 0 (mod 17). (iv) Substitute w = 17w₁ and divide by 17², giving the form 17w₁² = 1 + 12·17z₁²·…, so mod 17, 0 ≡ 1, a contradiction. (There's more than one way to write it, but alternating parity of valuations with mod 17 is the skeleton.)

Problem 3★★ Standard

Carry out rigorously the part of Section 06 in the text described as "the rest is handled automatically by the group structure". That is, from S(φ)(E/ℚ) being a subgroup of ℚ(S,2) = {±1,±2,±17,±34} containing {1,−1,2,−2} and not containing 17, conclude S(φ) = {±1,±2}. Also state in one line why S(φ) is a subgroup (the cohomological reason).

Open the solution

S(φ) is the kernel of the group homomorphism H¹(G,E[φ]) → ∏vWC(E/Kv), so it's a subgroup (that's the one-line reason. In the language of homogeneous spaces alone the product structure is hard to see, but in cohomology it's obvious). {±1, ±2} ⊂ S(φ) is as in the text (−2 and 2 are in, their product gives −1, and 1 is the identity). If 17 ∈ S(φ), that contradicts the assumption. If 34 ∈ S(φ), then 34·2 = 68 ≡ 17 (mod squares) is in, a contradiction. If −17, then −17·(−1) = 17, a contradiction. If −34, then −34·(−2) = 68 ≡ 17, a contradiction. So all of {±17, ±34} are excluded, and S(φ) = {±1, ±2}. Order 4 is legitimate for a subgroup of a group of order 8 (Lagrange).

Problem 4★★ Standard

Check the recovery device ψ: for the rational point (z,w) = (1/2, 2) of C₂ in Section 06, check that ψ(z,w) = (d/z², −dw/z³) (d = 2) gives (8, −32), that this point really lies on E′ : Y² = X³ + 12X² − 32X, and that δ(8,−32) = 8 ≡ 2 (mod (ℚ×)²).

Open the solution

ψ(1/2, 2) = ( 2/(1/4), −2·2/(1/8) ) = (8, −32). On the curve: 8³ + 12·8² − 32·8 = 512 + 768 − 256 = 1024 = (−32)². ✓ The formula for δ (X.4.9) is δ((X,Y)) = X (X ≠ 0), so δ(8,−32) = 8 = 2·4 ≡ 2. ✓ The philosophy of Selmer groups, "if a global point is found for a d satisfying the local conditions, that d really does come from a rational point", is condensed in this small computation.

Problem 5★★★ Advanced

(Lind–Reichardt: a proof of the failure of the Hasse principleAEC X.4.11) For C : 2w² = 1 − 17z⁴, show the following.

(1) C(ℝ) ≠ ∅ and C(ℚ₁₇) ≠ ∅.
(2) C(ℚ₂) ≠ ∅, and C(ℚp) ≠ ∅ for p ∤ 2·17. (If needed you may use "the chart at z = ∞", 2w′² = z′⁴ − 17 (z′ = 1/z, w′ = w/z²).)
(3) C(ℚ) = ∅. (Hint: reduce to 2u² = t⁴ − 17s⁴ (integers, gcd(t,s)=1), show that 17 is a quadratic residue mod p for each prime factor p of u, derive (p/17) = 1 by quadratic reciprocity, so that all of u is a quadratic residue mod 17, and hence conclude that 2 is a fourth-power residue mod 17, giving a contradiction.)

Open the outline of the solution

(1) ℝ: if |z| is small then 1 − 17z⁴ > 0, so w can be found. ℚ₁₇: with z = 0, w² = 1/2. 1/2 ≡ 9 (mod 17) is a quadratic residue (9 = 3²), so by Hensel's lemma it lifts to w ∈ ℤ₁₇.

(2) ℚ₂: try z = 1/11 ∈ ℤ₂ (11 is a 2-adic unit): 1 − 17/11⁴ = 14624/14641, w² = 7312/11⁴ = 16·457/11⁴. 457 ≡ 1 (mod 8), so 457 is a square in ℤ₂, hence w = 4√457/121 ∈ ℚ₂ exists. p ∤ 2·17: the reduction of C is a smooth curve of genus 1 over 𝔽p (think of the projective model gluing the two charts). By the Hasse–Weil bound #C(𝔽p) ≥ p + 1 − 2√p > 0 there is an 𝔽p-point, and by smoothness it lifts by Hensel. Small p can also be checked by hand (e.g. for p = 5 the z-chart has no solution, but the z′-chart has (z′,w′) = (0, 2): 2·4 = 8 ≡ −17 ≡ 3 … is the check −2 ≡ 3. Having to look at both charts is the educational trap of this problem).

(3) Suppose there's a rational point (z,w); set z = s/t (in lowest terms) and clear denominators: 2u² = t⁴ − 17s⁴ (we may take u = wt² ∈ ℤ, and u > 0: if u = 0, 17 would be a fourth power, a contradiction). gcd(t,s) = 1. 17 ∤ u: if 17 | u then t⁴ ≡ 0 (mod 17), so 17 | t, and then 17⁴ | 17s⁴ gives 17 | s, contradicting the gcd. Any odd prime factor p of u (p ≠ 17): t⁴ ≡ 17s⁴ (mod p) and p ∤ s (otherwise p | t), so 17 ≡ (t/s)⁴: 17 is a fourth-power residue mod p, in particular a quadratic residue. Since 17 ≡ 1 (mod 4), reciprocity gives (p/17) = (17/p) = 1. Also (2/17) = 1 (6² = 36 ≡ 2) and (−1/17) = 1 (17 ≡ 1 mod 4). So every factor of u = ±2e·∏pi is a quadratic residue mod 17, and so is u itself: u ≡ g2c (g a primitive root mod 17). Then from 2u² ≡ t⁴ (mod 17), 2 ≡ t⁴·u⁻² = (fourth-power residue)·(fourth-power residue)⁻¹ is a fourth-power residue. But the fourth-power residues mod 17 are {1, 4, 13, 16}, and 2 is not among them (2 has order 8). A contradiction.

In summary: C has points over every local field but no ℚ-points. C gives a nontrivial element of order 2 in Ш of the corresponding elliptic curve (its Jacobian).

Problem 6★★★ Advanced

(Selmer groups as an upper bound for rank) From the exact sequence for φ = [2], 0 → E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) → S(2)(E/ℚ) → Ш(E/ℚ)[2] → 0, derive the ℤ/2-dimension equation

dim₂ S(2) = rank E(ℚ) + dim₂ E(ℚ)[2] + dim₂ Ш[2]

. Further, accepting the Cassels pairing (X.4.14), derive "if Ш is finite, #Ш is a perfect square" (Hint: think about the order of a finite abelian group with a nondegenerate alternating form).

Open the outline of the solution

First half: from E(ℚ) ≅ ℤʳ × T (T finite), E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/2)ʳ × T/2T, and dim₂ T/2T = dim₂ T[2] (for a finite abelian group, the cokernel of 2 and the 2-torsion have the same dimension: check cyclic component by cyclic component via the structure theorem). So dim₂ E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) = r + dim₂ E(ℚ)[2]. Counting dimensions in the exact sequence gives the claimed equation directly. This equation is the precise meaning of "Selmer group = a computable upper bound for rank": dim₂ S(2) − dim₂ E(ℚ)[2] ≥ r, with equality ⟺ Ш[2] = 0.

Second half: if Ш is finite, the Cassels pairing Γ is nondegenerate on Ш (in a finite group the only divisible element is 0). A finite abelian group A with a nondegenerate alternating bilinear form Γ : A × A → ℚ/ℤ has the form A ≅ B × B, by constructing a symplectic basis (take each cyclic pair (x, y) with Γ(x,y) = 1/n and induct down to the orthogonal complement). So #A = (#B)². The same argument goes through for each p-component, so the order of each p-part is a square too.
