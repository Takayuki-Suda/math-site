The Algebraic Number Theory Arsenal
A Neukirch Companion for the Series

This series (Part 1 to the final chapter) borrowed several big theorems of algebraic number theory "as a user". This appendix is a catalog recording exactly where those borrowed items are stored. It's not a page to read straight through. When you're rereading the series and think "where can I learn more about this tool?", open it and jump to the relevant section of Neukirch ── it's a reference-only arsenal for that.

TEXT: J. Neukirch, Algebraic Number Theory, Grundlehren 322, Springer (ANT below)

Section 01

How to use this page

There are three intended uses.

  1. When you get stuck while rereading: when you meet an outside theorem in the series, like "finiteness of the class number" or "Hensel's lemma", and want to check what's inside, jump from the master table in the next section to the chapter and section of ANT.
  2. Browse the shelves to find weak spots: the seven "shelves" from Section 03 on arrange the knowledge the series actually used by theme. From each shelf's "where it's used in the series", you can go back to the scene where that tool did its work.
  3. Stocking up for the next climb: before moving on to ATAEC (especially CM and class field theory), previewed in the final chapter, use the companion route in Section 10 to gather the gear you're missing all at once.
Chip colors and how to read the numbers

On this page three books are distinguished by color: AEC VIII.1.6 = Silverman GTM 106, ATAEC II.10.5 = Silverman GTM 151, ANT I.6.3 = Neukirch Grundlehren 322. "I.6.3" in ANT means "(6.3) in Chapter I §6". References only down to the section (e.g. ANT II §7) are places where I recommend "reading the whole section".

⏩ A note to perfectionists (also valid for this appendix)

ANT is a big book of about 570 pages, and includes high ground this series doesn't need, such as the Riemann–Roch theory (III §3–§8) and the axiomatics of abstract class field theory (IV §4–§7). Sections not listed in this arsenal aren't needed to understand the series. Open only the shelf you need, when you need it ── use it the same way you read the main series.

Shelf 01 ─ ANT I §2–§3, §8–§10 / III §2

Shelf 1: Dedekind rings, prime ideal factorization, ramification

"There are only finitely many bad primes", "you can see whether it's unramified from the discriminant" ── the foundation of the foundation of the series. The ring of integers OK of a number field K is a Dedekind ring, and ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals ANT I §2–§3. How prime ideals behave in an extension L/K (splitting, inertia, ramification, the fundamental identity Σeifi = n) is ANT I §8; decomposition groups, inertia groups and Frobenius in Galois extensions are ANT I §9.

Ramification is detected by the discriminant ANT III.2.12

A prime ideal 𝔭 of K ramifies in L ⟺ 𝔭 divides the discriminant 𝔡L/K. In particular only finitely many primes ramify.

Cyclotomic fields are the most important examples: the ring of integers of ℚ(ζn) is ℤ[ζn] ANT I.10.2, the splitting law for primes is ANT I.10.3, and essentially only the primes dividing n ramifyANT I.10.4.

⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Part 2 STEP 2: that S = {bad reduction}∪{v|m}∪∞ is a finite set, and what the phrase "unramified at v" really means.
  • Part 2 example: the discriminant of L = ℚ(i,√2) = ℚ(ζ₈) is a power of 2, and only 2 ramifies ── checking it against the theory's prediction is exactly I.10.4.
  • Part 2 STEP 3(iii): the general theory behind the argument that derives, from the discriminant ±mmam−1 of Xm − a, the unramified condition ordv(a) ≡ 0 (mod m).
Shelf 02 ─ ANT I §5–§7, §11 / III §2

Shelf 2: the two great finiteness theorems and Hermite–Minkowski

The shelf that supports "the arithmetic heart" of the weak Mordell–Weil theorem (Part 2 STEP 3). Both proofs come from Minkowski's lattice point theorem (the geometry of numbersANT I §4–§5).

Finiteness of the class number ANT I.6.3 / the unit theorem ANT I.7.4 / the S-unit theorem ANT I.11.7
ClK is a finite group / OK× ≅ μ(K) × ℤr+s−1 / OS× ≅ μ(K) × ℤ#S−1
r, s = the numbers of real and complex places. The third is the precise form of "RS× is finitely generated" used in Part 2
Hermite–Minkowski-type finiteness ANT III.2.13 / III.2.16

With the degree fixed, there are only finitely many extensions L/K unramified outside a finite set S (III.2.13). More strongly, there are only finitely many number fields of bounded discriminant (Hermite's theorem III.2.16).

Right after III.2.13, ANT adds a grand remark: "its analogue for curves is the Shafarevich conjecture, which became the basis of Faltings' solution of the Mordell conjecture". We meet the same idea again in AEC IX.6.1 (Shafarevich's theorem).

⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Part 2 STEP 3: finiteness of the class number in (ii) (enlarging S to get a PID), the S-unit theorem in (v). The scene where "the two great finiteness theorems are each used exactly once, in different roles".
  • Part 2, Problems 5 and 6: the isomorphism RS×/(RS×)m ≅ TS, and the alternative proof via Hermite–Minkowski (the theorem "assumed" in Problem 6 is really III.2.13).
  • Part 5, X.4.3: finiteness of cohomology unramified outside S = a replay of STEP 3. The ultimate source of the finiteness and computability of Selmer groups is this shelf.
Shelf 03 ─ ANT II §1–§9 / III §1

Shelf 3: the local world ─ completions, Hensel, inertia

A dictionary for "looking locally", the habit of the whole series. Valuations and completionsANT II §3–§4, p-adic numbersANT II §1–§2, the structure of local fieldsANT II §5. And two key theorems:

Hensel's lemma ANT II.4.6 (general theory in II §6)

Over a complete (more generally, Henselian) valuation ring, a simple root in the residue field always lifts. "If you can solve the congruence, you can solve it p-adically" ── the reason checking local conditions in Part 5 took only finitely many computations.

Unramified extensions and inertia ANT II §7 / II §9 • I §9

Unramified extensions of a local field are equivalent to extensions of the residue field (unique for each degree, generated by Frobenius). The inertia group Iv is defined as "the part acting trivially on the residue field", and L/K is unramified at v ⟺ Iv acts trivially.

The family of normalized absolute values and the product formula Πv|a|v = 1ANT III.1.3 are the reason heights over general number fields (HK in AEC VIII §5) don't depend on the choice of coordinates.

⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Part 2 STEP 2: "an element σ of the inertia group acts trivially on the residue field" ── at the crux of the unramifiedness proof, the language of this shelf is at work as is.
  • Part 5, worked computations: the valuation arguments for C₁₇(ℚ₁₇) = ∅ and C′₂(ℚ₂) = ∅, and the Hensel side of "for v ∉ S there are automatically local points" (Hensel + Hasse–Weil).
  • Part 4, the map of §5–§6: well-definedness of heights via the product formula.
  • All of the Ch. VII section in Part 1: the substructure of the language of reduction, E₀ and E₁.
Shelf 04 ─ ANT IV §1–§3

Shelf 4: infinite Galois theory, profinite groups, Kummer theory and Hilbert 90

The grammar for handling GK/K. In infinite Galois extensions, only "closed subgroups" correspond to intermediate fields (the main theorem with the Krull topologyANT IV.1.2). The view "profinite group = projective limit of finite groups"ANT IV §2 is what really lies behind the convention in Part 3 that "cocycles are only the continuous ones = only those factoring through finite extensions".

Hilbert 90 and Kummer theory ANT IV.3.5 / IV §3
H¹( GL/K, L× ) = 1 (for cyclic extensions, the classical form "norm 1 ⟺ of the form σ(β)/β")

As a consequence, when μm ⊂ K, the Kummer correspondence "abelian extensions of exponent m ⟷ subgroups of K×/(K×)m" is complete. ANT IV §3 treats this as "abstract Kummer theory", in the same framework as the later class field theory (Artin–Schreier theory sits on the same shelf).

⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Part 1, checklist #8: the precise meaning of "it's enough to think of GK/K through finite quotients".
  • Part 2 STEP 3(iii): "the maximal abelian extension of exponent m = adjoining m-th roots of all elements" is the Kummer correspondence itself.
  • Part 3, Section 04: via Hilbert 90, δ : K×/(K×)m ≅ H¹(G, μm). The reference point for the contrast "for elliptic curves H¹(G,E) doesn't vanish".
  • Part 3, Section 02: the convention of continuous cohomology.
Shelf 05 ─ ANT V §1–§5

Shelf 5: local class field theory ─ reciprocity, Hilbert symbols, formal groups

The support beneath Door 2 (the Tate curve, local duality) and Door 5 (CM) of the final chapter. At its center is one isomorphism:

Local reciprocity ANT V.1.3
G(L/K)ab ≅ K×/NL/KL× (L/K a finite Galois extension of local fields)
the norm residue symbol ( · , L/K). The conductor is defined in ANT V.1.6
  • Local Kronecker–WeberANT V.1.9: ℚpab is generated by roots of unity. The global version (abelian extensions ⊂ cyclotomic fields) comes right afterANT V.1.10. This is the prototype over ℚ of "Kronecker's youthful dream" told in the final chapter.
  • The Hilbert symbolANT V.3.1–3.2: a nondegenerate bilinear pairing K×/(K×)n × K×/(K×)n → μn. The prototype of "a nondegenerate pairing over a local field"; ATAEC Exercise 5.14's WC(E/K) ≅ Hom(E(K), ℚ/ℤ) and the Cassels pairing (AEC X.4.14) are relatives of this type.
  • Formal groups and Lubin–Tate theoryANT V §4–§5: the "formal groups" that controlled the reduction kernel E₁ of elliptic curves in AEC Ch. IV reappear here as tools that construct local class field theory itself. The view "cyclotomic fields = torsion points of the multiplicative group 𝔾̂m" is the local version of "CM = building class fields from torsion points of elliptic curves" (ATAEC II).
⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Final chapter, Door 2: the source of H²(Gv, Kv×) = Br(Kv) ≅ ℚ/ℤ (the fact ATAEC Exercise 5.14(c) cites "from local class field theory").
  • Part 5, nontriviality of Ш: the local duality at the source of the nondegeneracy of the Cassels pairing.
  • Where we meet AEC Ch. IV (the formal groups summarized in Part 1) again.
Shelf 06 ─ ANT VI §1–§8

Shelf 6: global class field theory ─ Hilbert class fields, the Hasse principle, reciprocity

This is the real body behind ATAEC II (CM) saying "the crash course in II §3 is enough". With the idele class group CKANT VI §1 as the star, global reciprocity ANT VI §5 gives the dictionary "abelian extensions of K ⟷ closed subgroups of CK".

Ray class fields and the Hilbert class field ANT VI.6.2–6.3 / VI.6.8

For any modulus 𝔪 there is a ray class field K𝔪/K, and every finite abelian extension is contained in some ray class field. The ray class fields of ℚ are nothing but the cyclotomic fieldsANT VI.6.7. The ray class field of modulus 𝔪 = 1 (the big Hilbert class field) is the maximal unramified abelian extension, and its Galois group is isomorphic to the (narrow) ideal class group. The Hilbert class field in ATAEC II.4.3's "K(j(E)) = the Hilbert class field" is exactly this.

  • The Hasse norm theoremANT VI.4.5: for cyclic extensions, "a norm locally everywhere ⟹ a norm globally". A representative case where the local–global principle holds, and right after it the Minkowski–Hasse theorem for quadratic forms is also mentionedANT start of VI §5. A point of comparison showing how delicate Ш ≠ 0 (the failure in genus 1) seen in Part 5 really is.
  • Power reciprocityANT VI §8: from the product formula for Hilbert symbols, Gauss's quadratic reciprocityANT I.8.6 and its n-th power version all come out at once. A section where you can see the historical origin of class field theory as "the theory of reciprocity laws".
⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Final chapter, Door 5: all the prerequisites of CM theory (Hilbert class fields, ray class fields, Artin reciprocity). Get your gear for reading ATAEC II from this shelf.
  • Part 5, Problem 5: the quadratic reciprocity used in the proof of Lind–Reichardt ((p/17) = (17/p), 17 ≡ 1 mod 4).
  • Part 5, Section 07: the background of "the Hasse principle holds in genus 0".
  • Part 2 example: why ℚ(ζ₈) is almost all of "the abelian extensions of exponent 2 unramified outside 2" can be seen through "ray class fields of ℚ = cyclotomic fields" (VI.6.7).
Shelf 07 ─ ANT VII §5–§8, §11, §13

Shelf 7: ζ and L ─ the class number formula, Hecke characters, Artin conductors

The analytic weapons for Doors 5 and 6 of the final chapter (L-functions of CM curves, BSD).

  • The Dedekind ζ and the analytic class number formulaANT VII §5 (Cor. 5.11 and right after): h, R and √|dK| appear in the residue of ζK(s). "A special value of an L-function = a product of arithmetic invariants" ── the prototype of the BSD conjecture. The right attitude is to read the refined BSD formula (#Ш, the regulator, Tamagawa numbers) as the elliptic-curve version of this class number formula.
  • Hecke characters and Hecke L-functionsANT VII §6 / VII.8.5: the general theory of Grössencharacters, and analytic continuation and the functional equation via theta series. The complete proof of the theorem ATAEC II.10.3 cites as "classically known" is here. Together with Deuring's theorem (the L of a CM curve = a product of Hecke L's), it's the reason BSD for CM curves "makes sense".
  • Artin L-functions and conductorsANT VII §10–§12: L-functions of Galois representations and Artin conductors. The conductor–discriminant formulaANT VII.11.9 is the general theory on the "f(E/K)" side of Ogg's formula (ATAEC IV.11.1). The conductor of an elliptic curve is the Artin conductor of the Galois representation called the Tate module.
  • Chebotarev's density theoremANT VII.13.4: Frobenius elements fill the Galois group uniformly. A theorem that's like the air of modern number theory, supporting statistics of curves reduced mod p (the distribution of ap, testing isogeny, and so on).
⚔ Where it's used in the series
  • Final chapter, Door 5: background to Deuring's theorem (analytic continuation of Hecke L).
  • Final chapter, Door 6: the general-theory side of the question "does L(E,s) even exist?". Modular forms (ATAEC I) and Hecke L (ANT VII §8) are two faces of the same functional-equation technique.
  • Final chapter, Door 1: the body of the definition of the conductor f(E/K) in Ogg's formula.
  • Part 4, §9: where the name "elliptic regulator" comes from (an analogue of the unit regulator R).

? Why Neukirch?

The theorems this series borrowed are all standard and can be found in other textbooks. I still recommend ANT because the viewpoint of going back and forth between local and global (exactly the viewpoint of Selmer groups) runs through the whole book, and because, through Riemann–Roch theory and an Arakelov-style viewpoint (Chapter III), it connects straight to the theme of the later part of this series, "number theory is geometry" ── height = intersection number (ATAEC III), Ogg's formula (ATAEC IV). More than an arsenal, it's also the trailhead for the next mountain range.

Every place in the main series where we said "⏩ accept it for now and move on" now has its repayment address spelled out. The courage to keep going on borrowed knowledge, and a map that lets you go back and repay anytime ── only with both can you walk fast and without worry. Have a good climb.
