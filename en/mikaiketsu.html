Elliptic curves ── where it all begins

A single curve brought down Fermat's Last Theorem, a 358-year-old problem, and it still sits at the center of a million-dollar unsolved problem. That is the elliptic curve.

Move a and b with the sliders below and watch the shape of the curve change.

y² = x³ − 2x + 2

When Δ = −16(4a³ + 27b²) = 0, the curve crosses itself and becomes "singular". Only smooth curves (Δ ≠ 0) are called elliptic curves.

Fermat's Last Theorem

xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ

"For n ≥ 3 there are no solutions in natural numbers. I have discovered a truly marvelous proof, which this margin is too narrow to contain." ── the line Fermat left in the margin of a book in 1637.

The conjecture of Yutaka Taniyama and Goro Shimura linked elliptic curves and modular forms, and Andrew Wiles shut himself in his attic for seven years to complete the proof. Walls are only broken by people who keep asking.

The Riemann Hypothesis

ζ(s) = 0 ⇒ Re(s) = ½

Primes seem to appear at random. But behind them lies perfect order ── the 1859 conjecture that all the zeros of the zeta function lie on a single straight line. Prove it, and the distribution of primes is completely in your hands. For over 165 years, no one has beaten it.

UnsolvedMillennium Prize $1 million

The BSD Conjecture (Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture)

y² = x³ + ax + b

How many rational points (points you can write with fractions) lie on an elliptic curve? Infinitely many, or do they run out? ── The conjecture says the answer is engraved in the behavior of each curve's own "L-function".

An even deeper abyss of the elliptic curves that defeated Fermat. And it's also the world that Mr. Suda, the creator of this site, is still researching today.
