Tracing the "cracks" in high school math

Is this proof
really correct?

limx→0 (sin x / x) = 1

That proof you learned in high school. Draw a picture, squeeze it, and "look, it's 1".
But wait. A quiet contradiction is hiding inside that proof.

Section 01

The proof you learned in high school

High school math (Calculus)

First, let's check with numbers. How does sin x / x change as x approaches 0?

It really does approach 1. Now let's prove "why". Here is the textbook method.

▷ On the unit circle, when 0 < x < π/2:
　area of △OAB < area of sector OAB < area of △OAT
　(1/2)sin x < (1/2)x < (1/2)tan x
0.60 rad
cos x < sin x / x < 1

As x → 0, cos x → 1, so
by the squeeze theorem,　lim(sin x / x) = 1　□

Beautiful. Move x with the slider and you can see the sizes of the three shapes compare. It looks perfect.

Section 02

The crack in the proof

But stop here for a moment. This proof uses one thing "as if it were obvious".

Area of sector OAB　=　(1/2) × r² × x　=　x/2　(when r = 1)

Do you remember where you learned this formula?
It comes from "area of a sector = π r² × (central angle / 2π)".
And π comes from… the area of a circle.

⚠ A circular structure

The sector area formula　←　uses the circle area πr²
The circle area πr²　←　uses ∫√(1-x²) dx
∫√(1-x²) dx　←　is computed by a trig substitution
The substitution brings in　the derivative of sin
The derivative of sin　←　uses lim(sin x / x) = 1

── In other words, it assumes "the very thing we want to prove".

In high school we use "the area of a sector is just like this" as if it were obvious, but dig into it rigorously and lim(sin x/x) = 1 is already showing its face.

Another problem: what does "approach" mean?

"As x approaches 0, sin x / x approaches 1" ──
this word "approach" is actually never defined.
How close is "close"?
What does "gets arbitrarily close" mean mathematically?

High school settles it with intuition, but this is where the entrance to university mathematics lies.

Section 03

Defining "approach" with numbers
The entrance to the ε-δ method

University math (Analysis)

What does "f(x) converges to L as x→a" mean? At university, it is defined like this.

Definition (the ε-δ method)

For every ε > 0,
there exists δ > 0 such that
if 0 < |x - a| < δ,　then　|f(x) - L| < ε.

In that case we write　limx→a f(x) = L.

In words: "However small an error ε you are asked for, you can always find a closeness δ that meets it."

As you make δ smaller, you can see with your own eyes that it can handle any ε. That is the rigorous meaning of "convergence".

✓ A proof that avoids the circle (university version)

In university analysis, sin x is defined from the start as a power series (its Taylor expansion):
sin x = x - x³/6 + x⁵/120 - …

Then sin x / x = 1 - x²/6 + x⁴/120 - …
→ that it converges to 1 as x → 0 can be shown rigorously with ε-δ.

No sector areas, no π at all. The circle disappears.

Section 04

From high school to university math

"So was the high school proof wrong?"

It isn't wrong. It is intuitively correct and good enough in practice.
But when you try to pin down "why it is correct", you need definitions for your words.

What does "approach" mean?
Trying to answer that question leads you to the ε-δ method.
And the world of analysis opens up.

Mathematics is not finished in high school. In fact, high school math is designed as an entrance to deeper questions. The moment you think "Why?" is the beginning of university mathematics.
