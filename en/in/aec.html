The Arithmetic of
Elliptic Curves
─ The big map and prerequisites

A series of notes for reading J.H. Silverman's "The Arithmetic of Elliptic Curves" (2nd ed., GTM 106; AEC below) with a single aim: to truly understand Chapter VIII (the Mordell–Weil theorem). This first part gives the big picture of AEC, and collects from Chapters I–VII only "the gear you need to reach the summit".

Section 01

The goal of this series, and how to read it

There is one destination: AEC Chapter VIII, "Elliptic Curves over Global Fields", in particular to understand the following four sections, down to the details of the proofs and "why each argument is needed".

  • §1 The Weak Mordell–Weil Theorem
  • §2 The Kummer Pairing via Cohomology
  • §3 The Descent Procedure
  • §4 The Mordell–Weil Theorem over ℚ

Then, on the same line, we go as far as AEC Chapter X §4, "The Selmer and Shafarevich–Tate Groups", at the same depth. Parts 2–5 of this series correspond to those four high points.

Chapters I–VII, IX and the rest of X, on the other hand, are treated as summaries, as prerequisites and outlook for climbing to the summit. In the summaries the goal is "to be able to use the statements"; following the proofs is not required.

⏩ The most important promise of these notes

If you try to read AEC perfectly from page 1, you will almost certainly stall somewhere around Riemann–Roch in Chapter II. In these notes we explicitly mark the places where you should "accept it for now and move on". When a green box (like this one) appears, it's a signal that you don't need to stop there. Instead of understanding everything before moving on, work backward from the summit (Ch. VIII) and pick up only what you need. That's how to read this series.

The corresponding place in the original is always given in the form AEC VIII.1.5. "Proposition VIII.1.5" means "the 5th item in §1 of Chapter VIII", following the numbering of the 2nd edition of AEC. Whenever you want to know more, dive into that place in the original.

Section 02

Checking the goal ─ Mordell–Weil Theorem

Theorem (Mordell–Weil) AEC VIII, intro / VIII.6.7

Let K be a number field (a finite extension of ℚ) and E/K an elliptic curve. Then the group of rational points E(K) is finitely generated. That is,

E(K) ≅ E(K)tors × ℤr
E(K)tors: the finite torsion subgroup r: the rank

can be written.

"All the rational points of a cubic curve are generated from finitely many points by addition alone" ── the statement is one line, but the proof splits into two parts of completely different characterAEC VIII intro.

  1. The weak Mordell–Weil theorem: the quotient group E(K)/mE(K) is finite (VIII §1–§2). What's used here is algebraic number theory ── reduction, ramification, finiteness of the class number, the S-unit theorem.
  2. Descent: using a height function h, show that the whole group is generated by "representatives of E(K)/mE(K) + points of small height" (VIII §3–§6). What's used here is Diophantine geometry (the theory of heights).

This two-stage structure is a direct descendant of Fermat's "infinite descent". And when you push hard on "what gets in the way when you try to actually compute E(K)/mE(K)", the Selmer group and the Tate–Shafarevich group Ш appear (Ch. X §4, Part 5).

Section 04 ─ Summary

Ch. I Algebraic Varieties Algebraic Varieties

This chapter sets the stage. Over a field K (with a fixed algebraic closure K), it defines affine varieties, projective varieties and maps between them (rational maps and morphisms). There are only three things to hold on to.

  • Think of a variety as "a set of points over K + the information of which field it's defined over". Always distinguish V(K) (the K-rational points) from V = V(K). The Galois group GK/K acts on V(K), and its fixed points are V(K). This viewpoint takes center stage from Ch. VIII on.
  • The function field K(V), dimension and nonsingularity. For elliptic curves we only use the case "dimension 1, nonsingular".
  • On a projective variety, a rational map from a curve automatically extends to a morphism (it is defined at the smooth points of the curveAEC II.2.1). This is why the group law can be defined even at points where "the formula doesn't seem to make sense".
⏩ Don't stop here

No scheme theory is needed. AEC is written entirely in classical (Weil-style) algebraic geometry, and the naive understanding "variety = solution set of equations, morphism = map written by polynomials" gets you all the way to Ch. VIII. It's fine to move on even if the details of dimension theory or irreducibility are fuzzy.

Section 05 ─ Summary

Ch. II Algebraic Curves Algebraic Curves

The general theory of nonsingular projective curves. It's the groundwork for establishing the structure of elliptic curves in Ch. III. These are the results we use.

  • A nonconstant morphism between curves is surjective, a finite-degree "covering": if φ : C₁ → C₂ is nonconstant, it is surjectiveAEC II.2.3, and its degree deg φ = [K(C₁) : φ*K(C₂)] is defined. Ramification indices eφ(P), with Σ eφ(P) = deg φ (over each fiber)AEC II.2.6. This is what "the degree of an isogeny" really is.
  • Divisors: D = Σ nP(P), with degree deg D = Σ nP. A principal divisor div(f) has degree 0AEC II.3.1. Pic⁰(C) = (degree-0 divisors)/(principal divisors). This group is the "real body" of the group law on an elliptic curve.
  • The Riemann–Roch theoremAEC II.5.4: the genus g is defined, and ℓ(D) − ℓ(KC − D) = deg D − g + 1 holds. For elliptic curves (genus 1) we only use this consequence:
    When g = 1: deg D ≥ 1 ⇒ ℓ(D) = deg D
    This alone gives both "a genus-1 curve can be written with a Weierstrass equation" and "E ≅ Pic⁰(E)".
⏩ Don't stop here

The proof of Riemann–Roch can be a complete black box (AEC itself leaves the proof to other books). "ℓ(D) = the dimension of the vector space of functions whose poles are bounded by D, and for genus 1 and positive degree, ℓ(D) = deg D" ── if you can use this one line, you can follow Ch. III onward.

Section 06 ─ Summary (detailed)

Ch. III The Geometry of Elliptic Curves The Geometry of Elliptic Curves

Enter the star. The results of this chapter are used constantly in Ch. VIII, so we cover them more carefully than the other chapters.

(1) Weierstrass equations and the discriminant

An elliptic curve (genus 1 + base point O) can be written with a Weierstrass equation. If char K ≠ 2, 3,

E : y² = x³ + Ax + B, Δ = −16(4A³ + 27B²) ≠ 0
Δ ≠ 0 ⟺ nonsingular (an elliptic curve) j = 6912A³/(4A³+27B²) (the j-invariant)

The relation between nonsingularity and Δ is AEC III.1.4. In Ch. VII, Δ becomes the device that detects "at which primes the reduction goes bad".

(2) The group law ─ why it's associative

With the geometric addition "the three intersection points with a line add up to O", E(K) becomes an abelian groupAEC III.2.2. The coordinate formulas for addition (the addition and duplication formulas) are in AEC III.2.3(d), and the height computations of Ch. VIII §4 use these formulas directly. For y² = x³ + Ax + B:

x(P + Q) = ((y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁))² − x₁ − x₂ (x₁ ≠ x₂)
x(2P) = (x⁴ − 2Ax² − 8Bx + A²) / (4x³ + 4Ax + 4B) (duplication formula)

The "real reason" for associativity is that the map

E → Pic⁰(E), P ↦ the class of (P) − (O)

is a bijectionAEC III.3.4, and under this bijection geometric addition corresponds to the (trivially associative) addition in Pic⁰. The view "the group of an elliptic curve = a divisor class group" also pays off in constructing the Weil pairing and in the homogeneous spaces of Ch. X.

(3) Isogenies, dual isogenies, [m]

  • An isogeny: a nonconstant morphism φ : E₁ → E₂ sending O to O. It is automatically a group homomorphismAEC III.4.8.
  • The multiplication-by-m map [m] : E → E is an isogeny of degree m²AEC III.6.2(d). In particular, if m ≠ 0, [m] is nonconstant, and so on K-points it is surjective. This surjectivity is the starting point of the Kummer exact sequence in Ch. VIII.
  • Dual isogeny: there is φ̂ : E₂ → E₁ with φ̂∘φ = [deg φ]AEC III.6.1–6.2. This is the foundation for going back and forth between φ and φ̂ in "descent via 2-isogeny" in Ch. X §4.

(4) The m-torsion points E[m] ─ the star of Ch. VIII

Theorem AEC III.6.4

When char K = 0 (or char K ∤ m)

E[m] := ker [m] = { P ∈ E(K) : [m]P = O } ≅ ℤ/mℤ × ℤ/mℤ

In particular E[m] is a finite group of order m², and GK/K acts on it (because the coordinates are algebraic numbers).

Note that the points of E[m] are in general not K-rational. "May we assume E[m] ⊂ E(K)?" becomes the first hurdle of Ch. VIII §1 (detailed in Part 2).

(5) The Tate module and the Weil pairing (know where they're used)

The Tate module Tℓ(E) = lim← E[ℓⁿ]AEC III §7 isn't used directly in Ch. VIII, but as "a linear-algebra object bundling the E[m]" it provides the language of Galois representations. The Weil pairing em : E[m] × E[m] → μmAEC III.8.1 is bilinear, alternating, nondegenerate and Galois-equivariant, and as a corollary if E[m] ⊂ E(K), then μm ⊂ K (the m-th roots of unity lie in K)AEC III.8.1.1. This is the basis for identifying E[m] with μm×μm in Part 3 (cohomology).

⏩ Don't stop here

III §7 (Tate modules), §9 (endomorphism rings) and §10 (automorphism groups) are not essential for Ch. VIII. It's fine to carry only E[m] ≅ (ℤ/m)² and the existence of the Weil pairing (and III.8.1.1) and move on. For the theory of isogenies, "[m] is a surjective homomorphism of degree m², and there is a dual φ̂" is enough for now.

Section 07 ─ Summary

Ch. IV Formal Groups The Formal Group of an Elliptic Curve

The chapter that looks at an elliptic curve near O through "power-series glasses". Taking the local coordinate z = −x/y at O, the group law can be written as a formal power series F(z₁, z₂) ∈ R[[z₁,z₂]]. This is called the formal group Ê of EAEC IV §1–§2.

For Ch. VIII, only one point matters. Over the maximal ideal M of a complete discrete valuation ring (e.g. ℤp), consider the group Ê(M) of points of the formal group:

Theorem (torsion in formal groups) AEC IV.6.1 / IV §6

Over a complete discrete valuation ring with residue characteristic p, Ê(M) "has no torsion of order prime to p". That is, if m is prime to p, there are no points of order m in Ê(M).

This is the tool for proving in Ch. VII that "the reduction map is injective on m-torsion" (VII.3.1, below), and that VII.3.1 is exactly what's used at the crux of the proof of the weak Mordell–Weil theorem (the unramifiedness argument). The chain of dependence is IV§6 → VII.3.1 → VIII.1.5b.

⏩ Don't stop here

You can skip the general theory of formal groups (formal logarithms, heights, characteristic p). "E₁ (the points that reduce to O) is controlled by the formal group, and it has no torsion prime to p" ── carry just this conclusion and move on.

Section 08 ─ Summary

Ch. V Over Finite Fields / Ch. VI Over ℂ Finite Fields / Complex Numbers

Ch. V ─ Elliptic curves over finite fields

The centerpiece is Hasse's bound: for E/𝔽q,

| #E(𝔽q) − q − 1 | ≤ 2√q
[AEC V.1.1] "The number of rational points differs from q+1 by at most 2√q"

It isn't used in the proof of Ch. VIII itself, but it's the background for getting a feel for the size of "the reduced curve Ẽ(k)" in Ch. VII, and for guaranteeing the existence of 𝔽p-points in Part 5 (computing local conditions for Selmer groups).

Ch. VI ─ Elliptic curves over ℂ (lattices and uniformization)

Over ℂ there is an analytic isomorphism E(ℂ) ≅ ℂ/Λ (Λ a lattice) (the uniformization theoremAEC VI §5). Then the m-torsion points

E[m] ≅ (1/m)Λ / Λ ≅ ℤ/mℤ × ℤ/mℤ

become visible to the eye. This is the best intuition for the structure given by the algebraic proof of III.6.4. Cut the parallelogram (fundamental domain) into an m×m grid, and the grid points are E[m].

⏩ Don't stop here

Ch. V and VI are not logical prerequisites for the proofs in Ch. VIII (AEC itself is organized so that "V and VI may be skipped"). Once you have the intuition for E[m] ≅ (ℤ/m)², that's enough.

Section 09 ─ Summary (detailed)

Ch. VII Elliptic Curves over Local Fields Elliptic Curves over Local Fields

Right before Ch. VIII, this is the most important prerequisite chapter. Let K be a local field (e.g. a finite extension of ℚp), R its ring of integers, k the residue field and π a uniformizer.

(1) Minimal Weierstrass equations and reduction

Changing coordinates to put the coefficients in R and making v(Δ) as small as possible gives a minimal Weierstrass equation (it existsAEC VII.1.3). Reducing the coefficients mod π gives a (possibly singular) curve Ẽ over k, and the reduction map E(K) → Ẽ(k) is defined. Then:

  • Good reduction: Ẽ is nonsingular (⟺ v(Δ) = 0 for a minimal equation).
  • Bad reduction: Ẽ is singular. A node gives multiplicative reduction, a cusp gives additive reductionAEC VII §5.

There is an important filtration by subgroupsAEC VII.2.1–2.2:

E₁(K) ⊂ E₀(K) ⊂ E(K)
E₀ = points whose reduction is a nonsingular point E₁ = points whose reduction is Õ (the kernel of reduction)
0 → E₁(K) → E₀(K) → Ẽns(k) → 0 (exact sequence)
E₁(K) ≅ Ê(M) (isomorphic to the points of the formal group)

Moreover [E(K) : E₀(K)] is finite (Kodaira–NéronAEC VII.6.1–6.2; knowing the name is enough).

(2) The theorem at the crux of weak Mordell–Weil

Theorem (reduction is injective on torsion) AEC VII.3.1

If E/K has good reduction and m is prime to the residue characteristic char k, the reduction map is injective on the m-torsion:

E(K)[m] ↪ Ẽ(k)

(Core of the proof: the kernel E₁(K) ≅ Ê(M) has no torsion prime to p = char k [IV §6].)

This theorem is the only nontrivial input to "the extension K([m]⁻¹E(K))/K is unramified outside S"AEC VIII.1.5b, proved in Part 2. You could almost say this is the one weapon to take from Ch. VII.

(3) The Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich criterion (outlook)

The criterion "good reduction ⟺ the inertia group acts trivially on the Tate module (or on enough m-torsion points)"AEC VII.7.1. As a corollary, "isogenous curves have good reduction at the same primes"AEC VII.7.2. The latter is quietly used in Part 5 (when taking the set of bad primes of a φ-isogeny for the finiteness of Selmer groups).

⏩ Don't stop here

Once you understand the statement of VII.3.1 (and the one line about "why formal groups work"), you can enter Ch. VIII. The Kodaira–Néron classification, Tate's algorithm and the details of E/E₀ aren't needed for this climb.
