The Kummer Pairing via Cohomology

Put Part 2's "bare-handed proof" onto the shelving system called Galois cohomology, and it becomes a single exact sequence. This restatement itself isn't essential for the weak MW theorem ── but the Selmer group and Ш (Part 5) can only be written in this language. This is the entrance to Chapter X.

Section 01

Why restate it?

In the argument we saw in Part 2, "familiar-looking forms" actually kept appearing. The map λP(σ) = Qσ − Q, the computational rule κ(P,στ) = κ(P,σ)τ + κ(P,τ), and the phenomenon "changing Q produces a shift σ ↦ Tσ − T". These are all standard vocabulary of group cohomology (1-cocycles, coboundaries).

There are three things gained by this restatement.

  • The assumption E[m] ⊂ E(K) is no longer needed: for general E/K an injection E(K)/mE(K) ↪ H¹(GK/K, E[m]) can be defined.
  • You can see "what the obstruction is": for elliptic curves H¹(G, E) ≠ 0, and this nonzero group becomes the star of Part 5 (the home of Ш).
  • A framework comparing local and global: cohomology goes well with changing the field (K → Kv), and "cutting the global by local conditions" = the definition of Selmer groups can be written down as is.
⏩ The only tools needed are H⁰ and H¹

The only cohomology used in these notes is H⁰ and H¹ (AEC too only treats H⁰ and H¹ in Appendix B). No general theory such as derived functors or spectral sequences is needed. The "crash course" in the next section covers everything.

Section 02

Crash course in group cohomology ─ H⁰ and H¹ AEC App. B §1–§2

Let G be a group and M an abelian group on which G acts (from the left) (a G-module).

Definition (H⁰, 1-cocycles, H¹)
H⁰(G, M) = MG = { m ∈ M : mσ = m (∀σ) } (the invariant part)

A map c : G → M (σ ↦ cσ) is a 1-cocycle if:

cστ = cστ + cτ (the cocycle condition)

Those that can be written cσ = mσ − m for some m ∈ M are called 1-coboundaries. And:

H¹(G, M) = (1-cocycles)/(1-coboundaries)

There are two basic facts you can check right away (they're also Exercises 1 and 2).

  • If the action of G is trivial, the cocycle condition is cστ = cσ + cτ, and the only coboundary is 0. So H¹(G, M) = Hom(G, M) (all group homomorphisms).
  • For profinite groups such as G = GK/K, we only consider continuous cocycles (= those factoring through some finite Galois extension). From now on this convention is implicitAEC App. B §2.

Short exact sequence → long exact sequence (low degrees)

From a short exact sequence of G-modules 0 → A → B → C → 0, the following exact sequence sprouts:

0 → AG → BG → CG →δ H¹(G,A) → H¹(G,B) → H¹(G,C)

The key is how to build the connecting homomorphism δ. Take c ∈ CG and choose one lift b in B (image of b = c). Then σ ↦ bσ − b is a 1-cocycle with values in A (because its image in C is cσ − c = 0), and its class is δ(c). Changing the lift b only produces a difference of coboundaries, so the class is well defined.

? δ measures "the obstruction to division"

δ(c) = 0 ⟺ b can be corrected by an element of A to be G-invariant ⟺ c comes from BG. That is, δ is the obstruction to "can the invariant element c be lifted to B while staying invariant?". A device that measures the mismatch "the element exists, but no Galois-invariant element exists" ── you could say this is the entire role of cohomology in number theory.

Section 03

The Kummer exact sequence for elliptic curves

Fix m ≥ 2 and let G = GK/K. [m] : E(K) → E(K) is surjective (K is algebraically closed, [m] is a nonconstant isogeny), and its kernel is E[m]. So there is a short exact sequence of G-modules

0 → E[m] → E(K) →[m] E(K) → 0

. Taking the long exact sequenceAEC VIII §2:

0 → E(K)[m] → E(K) →[m] E(K) →δ H¹(G, E[m]) → H¹(G, E(K)) →[m] H¹(G, E(K))

Cutting out the middle gives the Kummer exact sequence for elliptic curves:

The Kummer exact sequence AEC VIII §2
0 → E(K)/mE(K) →δ H¹(G, E[m]) → H¹(G, E(K))[m] → 0

Moreover, the cocycle representation of δ, choosing Q with [m]Q = P, is

δ(P) = [ σ ↦ Qσ − Q ] = [ σ ↦ κ(P, σ) ]

That is, the connecting homomorphism δ is exactly the Kummer pairing of Part 2.

It's just the general construction of δ from the previous section applied to B = E(K), C = E(K), A = E[m]. Part 2's intuition, "the Galois twist when trying to divide P by m", matched exactly the general theory of "the connecting homomorphism measuring the obstruction to division".

And (a)(b)(c) of Proposition VIII.1.2 in Part 2 come out automatically from the general theory of cohomology:

  • (a) well-definedness → well-definedness of δ (a difference of coboundaries doesn't affect the class). The assumption E[m] ⊂ E(K) is no longer needed.
  • (b) bilinearity → δ is a group homomorphism + the cocycle condition.
  • (c) left kernel = mE(K) → exactness of the sequence (injectivity of δ).

Furthermore, when E[m] ⊂ E(K), G acts trivially on E[m], so H¹(G, E[m]) = Hom(G, E[m]), and Part 2's E(K)/mE(K) ↪ Hom(GK/K, E[m]) is recovered as is.

Section 04

Kummer theory for fields and Hilbert 90 ─ where is the decisive difference?

Let's do exactly the same procedure with the multiplicative group. From the exact sequence for z ↦ zm

1 → μm → K× →m-th power K× → 1

take the long exact sequence and cut out:

1 → K×/(K×)m →δ H¹(G, μm) → H¹(G, K×)[m] → 0

Here a monument of classical field theory comes into play.

Theorem (Hilbert's Theorem 90) AEC B.2.5
H¹( GK/K, K× ) = 0

Therefore the connecting homomorphism becomes an isomorphismAEC VIII.2.2:

δ : K×/(K×)m ≅ H¹(G, μm), δ(a) = [ σ ↦ ασ/α ] (αm = a)
Multiplicative group K×Elliptic curve E
Kernel of the sequenceμmE[m]
Target of δH¹(G, μm)H¹(G, E[m])
Obstruction group at the right endH¹(G, K×) = 0 (Hilbert 90)H¹(G, E(K)) ≠ 0 (in general)
Consequenceδ is an isomorphism. The theory "closes up"δ is only injective. A remainder is left
? The bottom right of this table is the reason Part 5 exists

For the multiplicative group, Hilbert 90 removes the obstruction, so "the theory of m-th roots" closes up in K×/(K×)m. For elliptic curves, H¹(G, E(K)) doesn't vanish. This group is later called the Weil–Châtelet group, and its elements have a geometric reality: "principal homogeneous spaces (torsors) of E". The part of it that is "locally trivial everywhere" is Ш (the Tate–Shafarevich group). In other words, all the stars of Part 5 are born from the one "≠ 0" cell of this table.

Section 05

What the reduction lemma really is ─ inflation–restriction

The reduction lemma of Part 2 STEP 0 (VIII.1.1.1) was also a special case of the general theory of cohomology. What we use is the inflation–restriction exact sequence.

Theorem (inflation–restriction) AEC B.2.4

If N ⊴ G is a normal subgroup and M a G-module, the following is exact:

0 → H¹(G/N, MN) →inf H¹(G, M) →res H¹(N, M)

With G = GK/K, N = GK/L (L/K finite Galois, with E[m] ⊂ E(L)), and M = E[m], we have G/N = GL/K, and we get the following commutative diagram:

Figure 1: what the reduction lemma really is. The top row is Part 2's exact sequence, the bottom row is inflation–restriction. The vertical arrows are injections coming from the Kummer exact sequence. The leftmost vertical arrow λ : P ↦ [λP] is exactly the map handmade in Part 2.

GL/K and E[m] are finite groups, so H¹(GL/K, E[m]) is a finite set (cocycles are maps from a finite set to a finite set). Φ injects into it, so it's finite ── the proof of the reduction lemma takes three lines. The injectivity of λP "computed by hand" in Part 2 was a restatement of the commutativity of this diagram + injectivity of inf.

⏩ The proof of inf–res can wait

The proof of inflation–restriction itself is a formal cocycle computation (treated in Exercise 4). On a first reading it's fine to just remember the usage "comparisons with finite extensions are done with this exact sequence" and move on.

Section 06

Unramified cohomology ─ the compressed form of the weak MW theorem

The "unramifiedness" of Part 2 STEP 2 can also be translated into the language of cohomology.

Definition (unramified cohomology classes) AEC VIII §2

Let v be a finite place of K and Iv ⊂ GK/K the inertia group (obtained by fixing one extension of v). ξ ∈ H¹(GK/K, M) is unramified at v if its restriction vanishes:

resIv(ξ) = 0 ∈ H¹(Iv, M)

(This doesn't depend on the choice of extensionAEC X.4.1.1 / Exercise B.6.)

Proposition AEC VIII.2.1

Let S = {bad reduction} ∪ {v(m) ≠ 0} ∪ MK∞. The image of δ : E(K)/mE(K) → H¹(G, E[m]) consists of classes unramified at every place outside S.

Proof

A representative cocycle of δ(P) is cσ = Qσ − Q ([m]Q = P). The proof of VIII.1.5b in Part 2 showed that "for v ∉ S, an element σ of the inertia group Iv fixes Q" (and that argument doesn't use E[m] ⊂ E(K)). So for σ ∈ Iv, cσ = O, i.e. the restriction of c to Iv is 0 as a cocycle.

∎

With this, the weak Mordell–Weil theorem is compressed into the following single line:

E(K)/mE(K) ↪ H¹(GK/K, E[m]; S) := { ξ : unramified outside S }
And the right side is finite (X.4.3, formally proved in Part 5. What's inside is the argument of Part 2 STEP 3 itself)
? "Cutting a global group with local conditions" ── the prototype of Selmer groups

H¹(G, E[m]) is a huge (infinite) group, but imposing conditions on "its behavior at each place" makes it finite. This way of cutting, "global cohomology group ∩ local conditions", is exactly the pattern of the definition of Selmer groups. In Part 5 we cut with local conditions more refined than "unramified" (the torsor is trivial over each Kv). Memorize the pattern here, today.

Section 07

If E[2] ⊂ E(K), everything can be written with square classes ─ laying the groundwork for Part 5

Let's see the benefit of the cohomological restatement in its most computational form. Suppose E[2] ⊂ E(K), that is,

E : y² = (x − e₁)(x − e₂)(x − e₃), e₁, e₂, e₃ ∈ K
T₁ = (e₁,0), T₂ = (e₂,0), T₃ = (e₃,0) are the 2-torsion points

. Choosing a basis T₁, T₂, E[2] ≅ μ₂ × μ₂ (as G-modules. μ₂ = {±1} has trivial action, so it's just (ℤ/2)²). Then by the Hilbert 90 isomorphism K×/(K×)² ≅ H¹(G, μ₂):

H¹(G, E[2]) ≅ K×/(K×)² × K×/(K×)²

That is, δ becomes "a map assigning to a point P a pair of square classes". What it really is turns out to be amazingly concrete.

The complete 2-descent map AEC X.1.4 (source of the rule) / X.1.1

The injective homomorphism E(K)/2E(K) ↪ K×/(K×)² × K×/(K×)² is

P = (x, y) ↦ ( x − e₁, x − e₂ ) (when x ≠ e₁, e₂)
(e₁, 0) ↦ ( (e₁−e₃)/(e₁−e₂), e₁−e₂ ), (e₂, 0) ↦ ( e₂−e₁, (e₂−e₃)/(e₂−e₁) )
O ↦ (1, 1)

given by these.

Why x − ei: the divisor of the function x − ei is 2(Ti) − 2(O), and the function realizing "the projection to the µ₂ component corresponding to Ti" is exactly this one (the rigorous derivation goes through the Weil pairingAEC X.1.1 and its proof. On a first reading it's fine to take it as "the μ₂ component of the twist in division = the square class of x − ei" and move on).

Example: E : y² = x³ − x = x(x−1)(x+1) (Part 2's curve again)

Let e₁ = 0, e₂ = 1, e₃ = −1. Computing the image of E(ℚ) = {O, (0,0), (1,0), (−1,0)}:

P(b₁, b₂) = image (square classes)Basis of the computation
O(1, 1)rule

Checking it's a homomorphism: in the group, (0,0) + (1,0) = (−1,0). In the image, (−1,−1)·(1,2) = (−1,−2). ✓

And recall the field L = ℚ([2]−1E(ℚ)) = ℚ(√−1, √2) computed in Part 2. The field obtained by adjoining square roots of the square classes −1, 2, −2 that just came out is exactly ℚ(√−1, √2). "The field of coordinates of the halving points" and "the square classes in the image of δ" are two faces of the same information ── here, bare-handed computation and cohomology mesh completely.

Exercises

Exercises

Problem 1★ Basic

For [m]Q = P (P ∈ E(K)), check that cσ = Qσ − Q satisfies the cocycle condition cστ = cστ + cτ. Also check that when Q is replaced by Q + T (T ∈ E[m]), the amount by which c changes is the coboundary σ ↦ Tσ − T.

Open the solution

cστ = Qστ − Q = (Qσ − Q)τ + (Qτ − Q) = cστ + cτ. (The middle equality is Qστ = (Qσ)τ and adding and subtracting terms.) As for replacement, (Q+T)σ − (Q+T) = cσ + (Tσ − T). σ ↦ Tσ − T is by definition the coboundary defined by T, so the cohomology class is unchanged. This is the cohomological version of the answer to Part 2, Problem 1.

Problem 2★ Basic

Prove that if G acts trivially on M, then H¹(G, M) = Hom(G, M).

Open the solution

If the action is trivial, cστ = cσ, so the cocycle condition becomes cστ = cσ + cτ, i.e. exactly that c is a group homomorphism G → M. The only coboundary is cσ = mσ − m = m − m = 0. So H¹ = (all homomorphisms)/(0) = Hom(G, M).

Problem 3★★ Standard

Assuming Hilbert 90 (H¹(G, K×) = 0), derive from the exact sequence the isomorphism δ : K×/(K×)m → H¹(G, μm), δ(a) = [σ ↦ ασ/α] (αm = a)AEC VIII.2.2. Also check directly that under this isomorphism "the class of a is trivial ⟺ a is an m-th power".

Open the outline of the solution

The long exact sequence of 1 → μm → K× → K× → 1 is K× →m-th power K× →δ H¹(G,μm) → H¹(G,K×) = 0. The right end vanishes, so δ is surjective, its kernel is (K×)m, hence an isomorphism. The formula for δ is exactly the general construction of the connecting homomorphism (lift α, σ ↦ ασ/α ∈ μm). Direct check: δ(a) is a coboundary ⟺ … ⟺ there is β ∈ K× with ασ/α = βσ/β ⟺ α/β is G-invariant ⟺ α/β ∈ K×. Then a = αm = (α/β)mβm, and βm is… adjusting the class of β gives a ∈ (K×)m (writing it out carefully is the exercise).

Problem 4★★ Standard

Prove exactness of the inflation–restriction exact sequence (B.2.4) by computing with cocycles. What you need to show is three things: (i) injectivity of inf, (ii) res∘inf = 0, (iii) ker(res) ⊂ im(inf). (N ⊴ G, M a G-module, inf the map pulling a G/N-cocycle back to G, res the restriction to N.)

Open the outline of the solution

(i) Suppose the pullback c of a cocycle c̄ : G/N → MN is a coboundary on G, i.e. cσ = mσ − m. For σ ∈ N, cσ = 0, so mσ = m (∀σ∈N), hence m ∈ MN. Then c̄ is the coboundary σ̄ ↦ mσ̄ − m on G/N.

(ii) The restriction to N of an inflated class is cσ (σ ∈ N) = the value of c̄ on N = c̄ē = 0. (A cocycle is 0 at the identity: from ce = ce·e = cee + ce.)

(iii) If res(c) = 0, then on N we can write cσ = mσ − m (σ ∈ N). Correct c by the coboundary σ ↦ mσ − m to get a cocycle c′ that is 0 on N. In the cocycle condition c′στ = c′στ + c′τ, taking τ ∈ N gives c′στ = c′στ: c′ is "almost constant" on each coset of N. Further, using it in the order σ ∈ N, τ ∈ G shows the values lie in MN, and one checks that c′ is the pullback of a cocycle G/N → MN.

Problem 5★★★ Advanced

Let E : y² = x(x−1)(x+1), K = ℚ. Use the table in the text (the image of the 2-descent map) to answer the following.

(1) Check that the four elements of the image {(1,1), (−1,−1), (1,2), (−1,−2)} form a subgroup of (ℚ×/(ℚ×)²)².
(2) Accepting that E(ℚ) has rank 0 (E(ℚ) = the four points above), E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) ≅ (ℤ/2)². Find the order of the right side of the injection E(ℚ)/2E(ℚ) ↪ Hom(GL/ℚ, E[2]) (L = ℚ(i,√2), Part 2), and confirm that this injection is not surjective.
(3) What does the "remainder" in (2) (the elements of Hom not in the image) mean? Anticipating the language of Part 5 (Selmer groups, Ш), state a conjecture.

Open the outline of the solution

(1) Just check it's closed under products of square classes: (−1,−1)·(1,2) = (−1,−2), (−1,−1)² = (1,1), etc. It's the Klein four-group of order 4.

(2) GL/ℚ ≅ (ℤ/2)² and E[2] ≅ (ℤ/2)², so Hom((ℤ/2)², (ℤ/2)²) ≅ (ℤ/2)⁴, of order 16. The image has order 4, so it's not surjective (16 > 4).

(3) "The elements of Hom that don't come from rational points" are exactly the source of the ineffectivity in Part 2, Remark 2 (VIII.1.8). In the language of Part 5: the group left after imposing all the local conditions is the Selmer group S(2)(E/ℚ), and its difference from the part coming from rational points is Ш(E/ℚ)[2]. For this curve, in fact, S(2) coincides exactly with the image and Ш[2] = 0 (computable by the methods of Part 5). That is, "the remaining 12 elements" are classes already excluded at the stage of local conditions.
