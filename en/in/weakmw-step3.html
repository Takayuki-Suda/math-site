Between-the-lines notes ─ Weak Mordell–Weil N3/N3

A complete proof of the field-theoretic finiteness
Finiteness from Class Numbers and S-Units

"Abelian extensions of exponent m unramified outside S are finite" ── we assemble this source of finiteness for the weak MW theorem step by step from Kummer theory, finiteness of the class number, and Dirichlet's S-unit theorem. Finally we connect N0–N3 and complete the whole proof of the weak Mordell–Weil theorem.

TEXT: Silverman, AEC 2nd ed. ─ VIII.1.6 (fully expanded) + assembling the theorem

Main II Weak MW N0 Reduction lemma N1 Kummer pairing N2 Unramifiedness N3 Finiteness from field theory
Section 01

The claim, and the gaps this note fills

Theorem AEC VIII.1.6

Let K be a number field, S ⊂ MK a finite set containing MK∞, and m ≥ 2. Let LK,S be the composite of all "abelian extensions of exponent dividing m that are unramified outside S". Then LK,S/K is a finite extension.

  • ① Checking that the definition of LK,S makes sense (the properties are preserved under composites)
  • ② (i) Reducing to adjoining μm ── a proof that unramifiedness is preserved under base change
  • ③ (ii) Line-by-line reasons for the procedure making RS a PID via finiteness of the class number
  • ④ (iii) A complete proof of both directions of the local criterion "Kv(m√a) unramified ⟺ ordv(a) ≡ 0 (mod m)" (the main part had just a word about the discriminant)
  • ⑤ (iv) A line-by-line expansion of the ideal computation aRS = 𝔞m ⇒ a = u·bm
  • ⑥ (v) Finiteness from the S-unit theorem, derivation of the equation [LK,S:K] = m#S (when μm ⊂ K), and a check with an example
  • ⑦ Assembling the whole weak MW theorem by connecting N0–N3
Section 02

Fixing the premises (K1–K6)

Sources: Neukirch (appendix Number theory arsenal), standard field theory textbooks, the R series of N2.

Premise K1 ─ the Kummer correspondence ANT IV §3 / Lang, Algebra VI §8

Let char K = 0 and μm ⊂ K. The correspondence Δ ↦ K(m√Δ) is

{ subgroups (K×)m ⊆ Δ ⊆ K× } ⟷ { abelian extensions M/K inside K of exponent | m }

is an inclusion-preserving bijection, with inverse M ↦ K× ∩ (M×)m. Moreover [K(m√Δ) : K] = (Δ : (K×)m) (if one is finite so is the other, and they're equal).

Open the outline of the proof (via Hilbert 90)

Two points are at the core. (1) A cyclic extension M/K (degree d | m) has, by Hilbert 90ANT IV.3.5, the form M = K(d√a) (an eigenvector of the generator σ: building θ with σθ = ζθ by a Lagrange resolvent gives θd ∈ K). (2) A finite abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups, so an abelian extension of exponent | m is a composite of cyclic extensions. From the nondegeneracy of the pairing Gal × Δ/(K×)m → μm, (σ, a) ↦ σ(m√a)/m√a, the equality of degrees and bijectivity follow. Details in ANT IV §3.

Premise K2 ─ finiteness of the class number ANT I.6.3

The ideal class group Cl(K) of a number field K is a finite group.

Premise K3 ─ Dirichlet's S-unit theorem ANT I.11.7

With s = #S, RS× ≅ μ(K) × ℤs−1 (μ(K) is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in K).

Premise K4 ─ structure of the ring of S-integers ANT I §11

For RS = { a ∈ K : ordv(a) ≥ 0 (∀v ∉ S) }:

  • RS is a Dedekind ring, whose prime ideals correspond one-to-one with 𝔭v (v ∉ S)
  • Fractional ideals factor uniquely as ∏v∉S 𝔭vnv (nv ∈ ℤ, all but finitely many 0), and aRS = ∏ 𝔭vordv(a)
  • RS× = { u ∈ K× : ordv(u) = 0 (∀v ∉ S) }
  • Cl(RS) ≅ Cl(K) / ⟨ [𝔭v] : v ∈ S ∖ MK∞ ⟩
  • A Dedekind ring has trivial class group ⟺ it's a PID
Premise K5 ─ the different and ramification ANT III §2 (III.2.12) / II §8

For a finite extension of local fields F′/F (rings of integers O′/O, F′ = F(α), α ∈ O′, g = the minimal polynomial of α over F ── its coefficients are automatically in O):

  • the different satisfies 𝔡F′/F | (g′(α))
  • F′/F is unramified ⟺ 𝔡F′/F = (1) ("ramification is detected by the discriminant = the different")
  • The ramification index of a global extension M/K at w | v can be computed in the completions: e(w/v) = e(Mw/Kv), and Mw = Kv(α) (α the image of a generator of M)ANT II.8.2
Premise K6 ─ two facts from field theory

(1) Galois group of a composite: if L/K is Galois and K′/K is arbitrary (all inside K), then LK′/K′ is Galois too, and the restriction map GLK′/K′ → GL/L∩K′ is an isomorphism. (2) Premises R2 and R3 from N2 (decomposition groups, inertia groups, the unramifiedness criterion via inertia fields) remain in use.

Section 03 ─ Preparation

The definition of LK,S makes sense (gap ①)

1

The composite of (any number of) extensions that are "abelian of exponent | m and unramified outside S" again has the same property. Hence LK,S is the largest such extension, and it's Galois over K.

▸ Reason Abelian, exponent: the product of restriction maps Gcomposite/K ↪ ∏i GMi/K is an injective homomorphism (if trivial on each component, it fixes all generators, so it's trivial). A subgroup of a product of abelian groups is abelian, and the exponent condition can be checked component by component. Unramified: for v ∉ S and an extension v̄, each Mi ⊂ Tv̄ (R3), so the composite is contained in Tv̄ too, and every finite subextension is unramified (the same argument as N2 Section 05 Step 1).

Section 04 ─ (i)

Reducing to μm ⊂ K (gap ②)

2

K′ := K(μm) is a finite Galois extension of K. S′ := { w ∈ MK′ : w|K ∈ S } is finite and contains MK′∞.

▸ Reason K′ is the splitting field of the separable polynomial Xm − 1 (char 0). There are finitely many places above a single placeANT I §8. Infinite places of K′ restrict to infinite places of K, and S ⊇ MK∞.

3

Setting L := LK,S, LK′/K′ is abelian with exponent dividing m.

▸ Reason K6(1): GLK′/K′ ≅ GL/L∩K′. This is a subgroup of GL/K, and inherits commutativity and the exponent condition.

4

LK′/K′ is unramified outside S′.

▸ Reason Take w′ ∉ S′, let v := w′|K (∉ S), and v̄ an extension to K lying above w′. L ⊂ Tv̄ (L is unramified outside S, R3). Also Iv̄/w′ = Iv̄/v ∩ GK/K′ (the tower relation in R2), so Iv̄/w′ fixes L (⊂ Iv̄/v) and fixes K′ (⊂ GK/K′). It fixes the generators, so it fixes LK′ too, i.e. LK′ ⊂ T′v̄ (the inertia field relative to K′). Reading R3 over K′, every finite subextension of LK′/K′ is unramified at every extension of w′. This is the complete proof that "base change preserves unramifiedness".

5

Therefore LK′ ⊆ LK′,S′. If the theorem holds for (K′, S′), then [L:K] ≤ [LK′:K′]·[K′:K] < ∞. From now on we may assume μm ⊂ K.

▸ Reason By Steps 3 and 4, LK′ is one of the extensions taking part in the definition of LK′,S′. Since L ⊆ LK′, [L:K] ≤ [LK′:K] = [LK′:K′][K′:K] (the tower law for degrees).

▸ In the context of weak MW, this reduction is actually unnecessary

When E[m] ⊂ E(K), μm ⊂ K follows automatically from the properties of the Weil pairingAEC III.8.1.1. That is, once N0's reduction (making E[m] rational) is done, the assumption of (i) is already satisfied. (i) is needed only when using this theorem on its own, detached from weak MW (as a statement for general K, S).

Section 05 ─ (ii)

Enlarging S to make RS a PID (gap ③)

6

If S ⊆ S₁, then LK,S ⊆ LK,S₁. So it's enough to prove it after enlarging S, keeping it finite.

▸ Reason The condition "unramified outside S" gets weaker as S gets larger. The family of extensions satisfying the condition grows, so the composite grows. Finiteness is inherited from above (the degree of a subextension ≤ the degree of the whole).

7

Take representative ideals 𝔞₁, …, 𝔞h of each class of Cl(K) (h = class number < ∞), and add to S all the places corresponding to their prime factors. Also add { v : v(m) ≠ 0 }. S stays finite after adding these.

▸ Reason h < ∞ is K2. Each 𝔞j has finitely many prime factors (prime factorization of ideals). There are finitely many v with v(m) ≠ 0 (N2 Section 03). A finite union of finite sets is finite.

8

For this S, Cl(RS) = 1, so RS is a PID. Also ∀v ∉ S : v(m) = 0.

▸ Reason K4: Cl(RS) ≅ Cl(K)/⟨[𝔭v] : v ∈ S⟩. Every class equals the class of some 𝔞j, and 𝔞j is a product of prime ideals in S, so it's trivial in the quotient. So the whole quotient is trivial. "Dedekind + trivial class group ⟹ PID" is the last item of K4.

Section 06 ─ (iii)

The description via Kummer theory, and the local unramifiedness criterion (gap ④)

Lemma N3-A (local unramifiedness criterion)

Let v be a finite place with v(m) = 0, a ∈ K×, M = K(m√a) (since μm ⊂ K, it doesn't depend on the choice of root). Then

M/K is unramified at v ⟺ ordv(a) ≡ 0 (mod m)

(⇐) Unramified when ordv(a) = mk

9

Take π ∈ K× with ordv(π) = 1 and set a′ := a/πmk; then ordv(a′) = 0 and M = K(m√a′).

▸ Reason Existence of π: ordv is surjective (R1). m√a′ := m√a / πk is a root of Xm − a′, and it and m√a are K-multiples of each other. So they generate the same field.

10

Take any w | v. Mw = Kv(α), α the image of m√a′. α is a unit: ordw(α) = 0.

▸ Reason Generation of the completion: K5. Computing the valuation: m·ordw(α) = ordw(a′) = e(w/v)·ordv(a′) = 0 (R1).

11

If g is the minimal polynomial of α over Kv, then g′(α) is a unit of Mw.

▸ Reason α is a unit (Step 10) and a root of Xm − a′, so it's integral, and hence g is monic with integral coefficients (the minimal polynomial of an integral element: a standard fact). Writing Xm − a′ = g·h (h also integral: Gauss's lemma), differentiate and substitute α: m·αm−1 = g′(α)h(α). The left side is a unit (v(m) = 0 and ordw(α) = 0). g′(α) and h(α) are integral (values of integral polynomials at an integral point), and ordw(g′(α)) + ordw(h(α)) = 0 with both ≥ 0, so both = 0.

12

Mw/Kv is unramified. Since w was arbitrary, M/K is unramified at v.

▸ Reason K5: 𝔡 | (g′(α)) = (1) (Step 11), so 𝔡 = (1), and "the different is trivial ⟺ unramified". Back to the global: e(w/v) = e(Mw/Kv) = 1 (K5).

(⇒) If unramified, then ordv(a) ≡ 0 (mod m)

13

If e(w/v) = 1 for w | v, then m·ordw(m√a) = ordw(a) = ordv(a). Since ordw(m√a) ∈ ℤ, m | ordv(a).

▸ Reason On K×, ordw = e(w/v)·ordv = ordv (R1, e = 1). Normalized valuations take integer values. The leftmost equality is (m√a)m = a and multiplicativity of valuations.

N3-A ∎

(iii) of the main theorem: LK,S = K(m√a : [a] ∈ TS)

TS := { [a] ∈ K×/(K×)m : ordv(a) ≡ 0 (mod m) ∀v ∉ S }
14

TS is a well-defined subgroup.

▸ Reason Changing the representative from a to a·cm changes ordv by m·ordv(c), invariant mod m. Subgroup: ordv is a homomorphism, so the condition is closed under products and inverses.

15

(The ⊇ direction) Letting ΔS be the pullback of TS to K×, K(m√ΔS) ⊆ LK,S.

▸ Reason K(m√ΔS) is an abelian extension of exponent | m by K1. Unramified outside S: for each generator m√a ([a] ∈ TS), N3-A (⇐) makes it unramified at v ∉ S (v(m) = 0 was secured in Section 05 Step 8), and the composite stays unramified by the argument of Section 03 Step 1. So the inclusion follows from the maximality of LK,S (its definition).

16

(The ⊆ direction) LK,S ⊆ K(m√ΔS). Therefore equality holds, and [LK,S : K] = (ΔS : (K×)m) = |TS|.

▸ Reason LK,S is abelian of exponent | m, so by K1 LK,S = K(m√Δ) with Δ = K× ∩ (LK,S×)m. For any a ∈ Δ, K(m√a) ⊆ LK,S (inclusion-preservation in K1) is unramified outside S (a subextension: the inclusion argument of R3). By N3-A (⇒), ordv(a) ≡ 0 (∀v ∉ S), i.e. Δ ⊆ ΔS. By inclusion-preservation in K1, LK,S ⊆ K(m√ΔS). The equality of degrees is the last claim of K1.

The remaining goal

Showing |TS| < ∞ gives the theorem. That's (iv)(v).

Section 07 ─ (iv)

Surjection from S-units (gap ⑤)

17

The map j : RS× → TS, u ↦ [u] is a well-defined homomorphism, with (RS×)m ⊆ ker j. So j̄ : RS×/(RS×)m → TS is induced.

▸ Reason If u ∈ RS×, then ordv(u) = 0 ≡ 0 (∀v ∉ S) (K4), so [u] ∈ TS. m-th powers lie in (K×)m, so their classes vanish. The universal property of quotients.

18

j̄ is surjective: for any [a] ∈ TS (representative a ∈ K×), in 18a–18d below we build u ∈ RS× with [a] = [u].

18a

The fractional ideal factorization aRS = ∏v∉S 𝔭vordv(a) (a finite product).

▸ Reason Uniqueness of factorization in K4.

18b

All the exponents are multiples of m (definition of TS), so 𝔞 := ∏ 𝔭vordv(a)/m is a fractional ideal with 𝔞m = aRS.

▸ Reason Dividing the exponents by m still gives integers (assumption). The ideal group is free on {𝔭v} (K4), so the equality is settled just by computing exponents. This is what's inside the main part's sentence "the condition ord ≡ 0 (mod m) means 'an m-th power as an ideal'".

18c

RS is a PID (Section 05 Step 8), so there is b ∈ K× with 𝔞 = bRS. So aRS = bmRS.

▸ Reason In a PID fractional ideals are principal too (as quotients of integral ideals). (bRS)m = bmRS.

18d

Setting u := a/bm, u ∈ RS× and [a] = [u]. Surjectivity is shown.

▸ Reason From aRS = bmRS, ∀v ∉ S : ordv(a) = m·ordv(b), i.e. ordv(u) = 0, and by the characterization of units in K4, u ∈ RS×. a = u·bm gives [a] = [u] in K×/(K×)m.

19

Bonus (j̄ is actually injective too): if u ∈ RS× is u = bm (b ∈ K×), then b ∈ RS×. That is, j̄ is an isomorphism RS×/(RS×)m ≅ TS.

▸ Reason ∀v ∉ S : m·ordv(b) = ordv(u) = 0 ⇒ ordv(b) = 0 ⇒ b ∈ RS× (K4). So ker j̄ is trivial. (This is the content of Exercise 5 in the main part. Thanks to it, the upper bound in the next section becomes an equality.)

Section 08 ─ (v)

Closing with the S-unit theorem (gap ⑥)

20

RS×/(RS×)m ≅ μ(K)/μ(K)m × (ℤ/mℤ)s−1.

▸ Reason K3: RS× ≅ μ(K) × ℤs−1. The m-th power quotient of a direct product is the direct product of the componentwise m-th power quotients. s−1 copies of ℤ/mℤ.

21

When μm ⊂ K, |μ(K)/μ(K)m| = m. Therefore |TS| = m·ms−1 = ms < ∞.

▸ Reason μ(K) is a finite cyclic group of order n with m | n (μm ⊂ K). In the cyclic group Cn the image of the m-th power map is Cn/gcd(m,n), and the quotient has order gcd(m,n) = m. This transfers to |TS| via the isomorphism of Step 19.

22

Together with Section 06 Step 16, [LK,S : K] = |TS| = ms < ∞. The proof of the theorem is complete.

▸ Reason In (i), general K was reduced to the case μm ⊂ K (then the degree is bounded by [K(μm):K]·ms′).

∎ (AEC VIII.1.6)
Checking ─ matching against the example in Section 08 of the main part

K = ℚ, m = 2, S = {2, ∞} (s = 2). The S-units are ±2k, so RS×/(RS×)² = {±1, ±2} ─ order 4 = 2² = ms. ✓ The corresponding field is ℚ(√−1, √2) = ℚ(ζ₈), of degree 4. ✓ The field computed in the main part's example as "L = ℚ(i, √2)" actually matches exactly the largest abelian extension of exponent 2 unramified outside {2,∞} ── the real thing behind the theoretical equation [LK,S:K] = ms.

? Exactly where the "division of labor" between class numbers and units lies

In terms of step numbers: finiteness of the class number is used only for 18c (lifting the ideal 𝔞 to an element b), and the S-unit theorem only for 20–21 (counting the m-th power classes of the remaining unit part). The two great finiteness theorems each appear exactly once, in different roles ── this is the rigorous form of what the main part called "a beautiful division of labor".

Section 09

Assembling everything ─ the weak Mordell–Weil theorem, zero-leap version

Theorem (weak Mordell–Weil) AEC VIII.1.1

K a number field, E/K an elliptic curve, m ≥ 2. Then E(K)/mE(K) is a finite group.

1

K₁ := K(E[m]) is a finite Galois extension (N0 corollary Steps 1–3). By the reduction lemma (N0 Steps 1–10), it's enough to show E(K₁)/mE(K₁) is finite. From now on replace K by K₁ and assume E[m] ⊂ E(K). Then μm ⊂ K also holdsAEC III.8.1.1.

2

Setting L := K([m]−1E(K)), L/K is Galois and there is an injection δ : E(K)/mE(K) ↪ Hom(GL/K, E[m]) (N1 Steps 14–17). Show [L:K] < ∞ and you win (N1 Step 19).

3

S := (bad reduction) ∪ (places dividing m) ∪ (infinite places) is a finite set, and L/K is an abelian extension of exponent | m (N2 Section 06) unramified outside S (N2 Section 05).

4

Therefore L ⊆ LK,S, and [LK,S : K] < ∞ (this note, N3). Hence [L:K] < ∞.

5

By N1 Step 19, E(K)/mE(K) is finite (for the extended K). Going back down to the original K with the estimate of N0 Step 4, E(K)/mE(K) is finite.

∎
