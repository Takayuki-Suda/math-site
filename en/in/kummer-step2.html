Hilbert 90 and the connection to the multiplicative group
Hilbert 90, the Weil Pairing, and Square Classes

We completely prove Hilbert 90, which the main part cited in one line as "a monument of classical field theory", from Dedekind's independence lemma to the profinite version (AEC B.2.5). Then, from the properties of the Weil pairing, we assemble E[m] ≅ μm × μm (when E[m] ⊂ E(K)), and go as far as why the "x − ei" map of §07 in the main part is a homomorphism ── an identity about lines ── building every bridge between E[m] and the multiplicative group.

TEXT: Silverman, AEC 2nd ed. ─ Ch. VIII §2, App. B.2.5, Ch. III §8, Ch. X §1 / Neukirch, ANT IV §3

Section 02

Fixing the premises (W1–W4)

Premise W1 ─ multiplicative notation and μm

K is a field of characteristic 0 (a number field in mind); K and G := GK/K are as in N1's D1. The star of this note, K×, is a multiplicative group, so we read N0's additive notation through the following dictionary:

Additive (N0, N1)Multiplicative (this note)
0, m + n, −m1, m·n, m−1
multiplication by m [m]m-th power z ↦ zm
cocycle condition cστ = cστ + cτcστ = cστ · cτ
coboundary σ ↦ mσ − mσ ↦ βσ/β

μm := { z ∈ K× : zm = 1 }. In char 0, Xm − 1 is separable (no common root with its derivative mXm−1), so #μm = m, and moreover μm is cyclic of order m (a finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic ── a standard fact about roots of unity).

Premise W2 ─ the correspondence of infinite Galois theory ANT IV.1.2

Open normal subgroups U of G and finite Galois extensions L/K inside K correspond one-to-one via U = GK/L (the main theorem with the Krull topology), and restriction gives G/U ≅ GL/Kweakmw N1 premise Q4. The fixed field is KU = L, and therefore (K×)U = L× (just removing 0).

Premise W3 ─ the Weil pairing AEC III.8.1

Let E/K be an elliptic curve, m ≥ 2, char 0. There is a pairing (the Weil pairing) with the following properties:

em : E[m] × E[m] → μm

(a) Bilinear: em(S₁+S₂, T) = em(S₁,T)·em(S₂,T), and likewise in the second variable. (b) Alternating: em(T, T) = 1 (hence em(S,T) = em(T,S)−1). (c) Nondegenerate: if em(S, T) = 1 for all T ∈ E[m], then S = O. (d) Galois equivariant: em(S, T)σ = em(Sσ, Tσ). The construction uses the theory of divisors and functions (AEC III §8). In this note we use only the properties.

Premise W4 ─ the machines of N0 and N1

The six-term sequence and exactness at 5 places (N0 §07), descending continuous cocycles to a finite stage (N0 §04), the cutting-out procedure and "induced map = multiplication by m (multiplicative version: m-th power)" (N1 Steps 9–12). Applying them to the multiplicative group goes through the dictionary in W1.

Section 03

The multiplicative group fits the machine's framework too (gaps ①②)

1

K× and μm are discrete G-modules.

▸ Reason σ is a field automorphism, so (ab)σ = aσbσ and 1σ = 1, i.e. each σ is a group automorphism of K× (R1(i) via the dictionary in W1). The composition convention (ii) and the identity (iii) are the field action itself. Discreteness: a ∈ K× is algebraic over K, so its stabilizer contains the open subgroup GK/K(a) (the same argument as N1 Step 2). μm is G-stable: (zσ)m = (zm)σ = 1σ = 1. It's finite (#μm = m, W1), hence discrete.

2

The following is a short exact sequence of discrete G-modules:

1 → μm → K× →m-th power K× → 1

▸ Reason z ↦ zm is a homomorphism (the multiplicative group is abelian). G-equivariant: (zm)σ = (zσ)m (a field homomorphism). The kernel is μm (definition). Surjective: for a ∈ K×, Xm − a has a root α in the algebraically closed field K, and since a ≠ 0, α ≠ 0, i.e. α ∈ K×. The inclusion μm ⊆ K× is a G-equivariant injection (G-stability in Step 1).

3

The six-term sequence (multiplicative version) and cutting out. Applying N0 §07 through the dictionary in W1 and substituting (K×)G = K×:

1 → μm(K) → K× →m-th power K× →δ H¹(G, μm) → H¹(G, K×) →(m-th power)* H¹(G, K×)

By the same cutting-out procedure as N1 §07:

1 → K×/(K×)m →δ H¹(G, μm) → H¹(G, K×)[m] → 1, δ(a) = [ σ ↦ ασ/α ] (αm = a)

▸ Reason (K×)G = K× is just KG = K (N1 D1) with 0 removed. μm(K) := μm ∩ K×. The formula for δ is the construction of N0 Step 11 with "an m-th root α" chosen as the lift (the multiplicative version of bσ − b is ασ/α)AEC VIII.2.2. That the induced map (m-th power)* agrees with "the m-th power of classes in H¹", and exactness at the 3 places of the cut-out sequence, follow from the proofs of N1 Steps 9–12 with just a change of symbols (the only properties used were "the operation on Z¹ is pointwise" and "exactness at 5 places"; additivity was never used).

Only one job remains: proving that the obstruction at the right end, H¹(G, K×), vanishes. This is Hilbert's Theorem 90. The key to the proof lies not in cohomology but in classical linear algebra.

Section 04

Dedekind's independence lemma (the key to gap ③)

Lemma (linear independence of characters: Dedekind–Artin) Lang, Algebra VI §4

Let H be a group, L a field, and χ₁, …, χn : H → L× distinct group homomorphisms (characters). χ₁, …, χn are linearly independent over L: if a₁χ₁(h) + ⋯ + anχn(h) = 0 for all h ∈ H, then a₁ = ⋯ = an = 0.

4

Assume a shortest nontrivial relation and derive a contradiction (minimal counterexample in n).

▸ Reason Among the nontrivial relations (coefficients not all 0), take one with the fewest nonzero coefficients. Dropping terms with coefficient 0 and renumbering, we may assume a₁, …, an ≠ 0. If n = 1, substituting h = e gives a₁χ₁(e) = a₁·1 = 0, a contradiction. So n ≥ 2. Since χ₁ ≠ χn, there is h₀ ∈ H with χ₁(h₀) ≠ χn(h₀).

5

Use h₀ to shorten the relation by one ── a contradiction.

▸ Reason For any h, substitute h₀h into the relation: Σ aiχi(h₀)χi(h) = Σ aiχi(h₀h) = 0. On the other hand, multiply the original relation by χn(h₀): Σ aiχn(h₀)χi(h) = 0. Taking the difference, the i = n term cancels: Σi≤n−1 ai(χi(h₀) − χn(h₀))χi(h) = 0 (∀h). The coefficient of the first term, a₁(χ₁(h₀) − χn(h₀)), is nonzero, so this is a nontrivial relation with fewer than n nonzero coefficients. This contradicts minimality. ∎

The form we apply: if L/K is a finite Galois extension, distinct τ ∈ GL/K are distinct characters L× → L× (they preserve multiplication), so they're linearly independent over L. That is, as long as the coefficients cτ ∈ L× are all nonzero, the function z ↦ Στ cτ·zτ is not identically 0.

Section 05

The finite version of Hilbert 90 (gap ③)

Theorem (Hilbert's Theorem 90, finite version) ANT IV.3.5

Let L/K be a finite Galois extension and G := GL/K. Then:

H¹( GL/K, L× ) = 1 (every 1-cocycle is a coboundary σ ↦ βσ/β)
6

Preparing to average: take c ∈ Z¹(GL/K, L×). For some a ∈ L×,

b := Στ ∈ GL/K cτ · aτ ≠ 0

▸ Reason The coefficients cτ are elements of L×, i.e. all nonzero. By the lemma of §04 (the form we apply), z ↦ Σ cτzτ is not identically 0 on L×, so there is a ∈ L× with b ≠ 0. The sum is finite (GL/K is a finite group), so b ∈ L.

7

Compute the Galois orbit of b with the cocycle condition: bσ = cσ−1 · b (∀σ).

▸ Reason bσ = Στ cτσ · aτσ (the action is a field homomorphism; (aτ)σ = aτσ is the right-action convention). Reading the cocycle condition (dictionary in W1) for the pair (τ, σ): cτσ = cτσ · cσ, i.e. cτσ = cτσ · cσ−1. Substituting, bσ = cσ−1 Στ cτσ aτσ. τ ↦ τσ is a bijection of GL/K (right translation in the group), so the sum equals b: bσ = cσ−1 b.

8

Conclusion: setting β := b−1, cσ = βσ/β. That is, c is a coboundary, and H¹ = 1.

▸ Reason βσ/β = (bσ)−1/b−1 = b/bσ = b/(cσ−1b) = cσ (Step 7). b ∈ L×, so β ∈ L×. Every cocycle is a coboundary, so H¹(GL/K, L×) = 1. ∎

? Relation to the classical form (the norm theorem for cyclic extensions)

When GL/K = ⟨σ⟩ is cyclic, a cocycle is determined by a single value cσ, and the cocycle condition turns into "NL/K(cσ) = 1". Then the theorem says "an element of norm 1 has the form βσ/β" ── the claim listed as "the classical form" on shelf 4 of ant.html. Hilbert's original paper (Satz 90 of the Zahlbericht) had this form. The b in the proof above is an averaging called a "Poincaré series", and the trick is to borrow the addition of the field to solve a problem about the multiplicative group.

Section 06

Lifting to the profinite version, and the isomorphism δ (gap ④)

Theorem (Hilbert 90, profinite version) AEC B.2.5
H¹( GK/K, K× ) = 0 (= 1 written multiplicatively)
9

Proof: drop a continuous cocycle to a finite stage and apply §05.

▸ Reason Let c be a continuous cocycle. K× is a discrete G-module (Step 1), so the theorem of N0 §04 applies: there is an open normal subgroup U such that c is the inflation of c̄ ∈ Z¹(G/U, (K×)U). By W2, U = GK/L (L/K finite Galois), G/U ≅ GL/K, (K×)U = L×. So c̄ ∈ Z¹(GL/K, L×). By the theorem of §05 there is β ∈ L× with c̄σ̄ = βσ̄/β. Inflation sends coboundaries to coboundaries: cσ = c̄σU = βσ|L/β = βσ/β (since β ∈ L, the action of σ is determined by its restriction σ|L). So [c] = 0. ∎

Consequence (the connecting homomorphism becomes an isomorphism) AEC VIII.2.2
δ : K×/(K×)m ≅ H¹(G, μm), δ(a) = [ σ ↦ ασ/α ] (αm = a)

The right end H¹(G, K×)[m] of the exact sequence cut out in Step 3 became 0, so the injection δ is also surjective. The "theory of m-th roots" for the multiplicative group closes up in K×/(K×)m ── every entry in the left column of the comparison table in §04 of the main part is now verified.

? Where the same proof fails to run for elliptic curves

The heart of the proof in §05 was the averaging b = Σ cτaτ, the operation "use elements cτ of the multiplicative group as coefficients and sum with the field's addition". E(K) has neither "multiplying by coefficients" nor "the field's addition" ── only addition of points. So the same averaging can't be defined, and in fact H¹(G, E) does not vanish in general (bottom right of the table in §04 of the main part). That one "≠ 0" cell is the expression of a structural reason: you can't average.

Section 07

Connecting E[m] to μm with the Weil pairing (gaps ⑤⑥)

From here on in this section we assume E[m] ⊂ E(K) (the same setting as §07 of the main part; for m = 2 it's equivalent to "all the ei are in K").

10

Corollary: if E[m] ⊂ E(K), then μm ⊂ K×. AEC III.8.1.1

▸ Reason Take a basis T₁, T₂ ∈ E[m] (E[m] ≅ (ℤ/mℤ)², N1 D3). Set ζ := em(T₁, T₂), and let d (d | m) be the order of ζ. For any S = [a]T₁ + [b]T₂, bilinearity (a) and alternation (b) give em([d]T₁, S) = em(T₁, T₁)da · em(T₁, T₂)db = 1 · (ζd)b = 1. By nondegeneracy (c), [d]T₁ = O. T₁ has order exactly m (it's a basis element), so m | d, hence d = m. That is, ζ is a primitive m-th root of unity and μm = ⟨ζ⟩ (W1: μm is cyclic of order m). By Galois equivariance (d) and Ti ∈ E(K), ζσ = em(T₁σ, T₂σ) = em(T₁, T₂) = ζ (∀σ), i.e. ζ ∈ KG = K. So μm = ⟨ζ⟩ ⊂ K×.

11

The map w : E[m] → μm × μm, S ↦ ( em(S, T₁), em(S, T₂) ) is an isomorphism of G-modules.

▸ Reason Homomorphism: bilinearity (a) in the first variable. Injective: if w(S) = (1,1), then for any T = [a]T₁+[b]T₂, em(S,T) = em(S,T₁)aem(S,T₂)b = 1, and nondegeneracy (c) gives S = O. Bijective: injective + both sides have the same order m² (#E[m] = m², #(μm×μm) = m²). G-equivariant: w(Sσ) = (em(Sσ, T₁), …) = (em(Sσ, T₁σ), …) = (em(S, T₁)σ, …) = w(S)σ (Ti is K-rational so fixed + equivariance (d)). Note that under the present assumption G acts trivially on both sides (E[m] ⊂ E(K), μm ⊂ K: Step 10), so equivariance also holds as "between trivial actions" ── what's inside the parenthetical in §07 of the main part, "as G-modules. μ₂ = {±1} has trivial action, so it's just (ℤ/2)²".

12

An isomorphism induces an isomorphism on H¹, and a direct product splits component by component. Conclusion:

H¹(G, E[m]) ≅w* H¹(G, μm × μm) ≅ H¹(G, μm)² ≅ ( K×/(K×)m )²

▸ Reason First isomorphism: the construction of f* (N0 Step 10) preserves composition and identity ── (g∘f)*[c] = [(g∘f)∘c] = g*f*[c], and (id)* = id is clear pointwise from the definition. So (w−1)*∘w* = (w−1∘w)* = id, likewise in the other order, and w* is an isomorphism. Second isomorphism: a cocycle of a direct product module is a pair of components c = (c′, c″), and the cocycle condition, coboundaries and continuity can all be read component by component, so the map induced by the projections, [c] ↦ ([c′], [c″]), is an isomorphism (the inverse is assembling the pair). Third isomorphism: the consequence of §06, used twice.

Consequence (the identification in §07 of the main part)

When E[m] ⊂ E(K), the δ of the Kummer exact sequence in N1 gives an injective homomorphism

E(K)/mE(K) ↪ ( K×/(K×)m )²

. For m = 2, μ₂ = {±1} ⊂ K always holds, so the only assumption is "e₁, e₂, e₃ ∈ K". This is the complete justification of H¹(G, E[2]) ≅ K×/(K×)² × K×/(K×)² in §07 of the main part.

Section 08

Making m = 2 concrete ─ why "x − ei" is a homomorphism (gap ⑦)

Let E : y² = f(x) = (x − e₁)(x − e₂)(x − e₃), ei ∈ K, Ti = (ei, 0). Why is the map φ = (φ₁, φ₂) of §07 in the main part, φi(P) = x(P) − ei (general points; special values by the rule in §07), a homomorphism? ── Everything rests on the following elementary identity.

Lemma (an identity about lines; verified with sympy)

Let y = λx + ν be the line through P₁, P₂ ∈ E (neither is O) (the tangent when P₁ = P₂), and P₃ := −(P₁ + P₂) ≠ O. Writing xj := x(Pj), for any e:

(x₁ − e)(x₂ − e)(x₃ − e) = (λe + ν)² − f(e)
In particular for e = ei (a root of f): (x₁ − ei)(x₂ − ei)(x₃ − ei) = (λei + ν)² = a square
13

Proof of the identity.

▸ Reason g(x) := f(x) − (λx + ν)² is a monic cubic. Chord case (x₁ ≠ x₂): P₁, P₂ are points of E on the line, so yj = λxj + ν and yj² = f(xj), hence g(x₁) = g(x₂) = 0. By the relation between roots and coefficients (comparing the x² coefficients) the third root is λ² + (e₁+e₂+e₃) − x₁ − x₂, which agrees with x(P₁+P₂) from the addition formulaAEC III.2.3. Since x(−P) = x(P), x₃ = x(P₁+P₂) is this third root. Tangent case (P₁ = P₂, y₁ ≠ 0, λ = f′(x₁)/(2y₁)): besides g(x₁) = 0, g′(x₁) = f′(x₁) − 2λ(λx₁+ν) = f′(x₁) − 2λy₁ = 0, so x₁ is a double root, and the third root is x₃ = x([2]P₁). In either case g(x) = (x − x₁)(x − x₂)(x − x₃). Now substitute x = e: g(e) = (e−x₁)(e−x₂)(e−x₃) = −(x₁−e)(x₂−e)(x₃−e). Rearranging gives the claimed formula. For e = ei, f(ei) = 0.

Rigorous verification with sympy (approach and results)

Chord: with x₁, x₂, y₁, y₂, e₁, e₂, e₃, e as indeterminates, set λ = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁), ν = y₁ − λx₁, x₃ = λ² + (e₁+e₂+e₃) − x₁ − x₂. Clearing denominators in the difference (x₁−e)(x₂−e)(x₃−e) − {(λe+ν)² − f(e)} and reducing modulo the relations y₁² − f(x₁), y₂² − f(x₂) gives identically 0. The displayed form with e = e₁, e₂, e₃ substituted is likewise 0.

Tangent: with λ = f′(x₁)/(2y₁), x₃ = λ² + (e₁+e₂+e₃) − 2x₁, reducing (x₁−e)²(x₃−e) − {(λe+ν)² − f(e)} by y₁² − f(x₁) → identically 0. We also confirmed the consistency of the special-value rule, (e₁−e₂)(e₁−e₃) ÷ {(e₁−e₃)/(e₁−e₂)} = (e₁−e₂)² (a perfect square).

14

φi is a homomorphism (general position). When none of P₁, P₂, P₁+P₂ is O or a 2-torsion point:

φi(P₁) · φi(P₂) · φi(P₁+P₂) = (x₁−ei)(x₂−ei)(x₃−ei) = (λei+ν)² ≡ 1

▸ Reason x(P₁+P₂) = x(−(P₁+P₂)) = x₃, so the left equality is a definition. The middle is Step 13 (if P₁, P₂ ∈ E(K) then λ, ν ∈ K: rational functions of the coordinates for a chord, f′(x₁)/(2y₁) for a tangent. If x₁ = x₂ and P₁ ≠ P₂, then P₂ = −P₁ and P₁+P₂ = O, which isn't this case). λei+ν ≠ 0 because "P₁+P₂ is not a 2-torsion point" (if it were 0, x₃ = ei would be a double or triple intersection). In K×/(K×)² every class is its own inverse (t² ≡ 1), so product ≡ 1 is equivalent to φi(P₁+P₂) = φi(P₁)φi(P₂). Cases involving O: trivial from φi(O) = 1 and P + O = P. Case P₂ = −P₁: φi(P₁)φi(−P₁) = (x₁−ei)² ≡ 1 ✓.

15

2E(K) lies in the kernel ── and the "square root" can even be written explicitly:

x([2]P) − ei = ( (λei+ν)/(x(P)−ei) )² (tangent y = λx+ν, x(P) ≠ ei)

▸ Reason Step 13 in the tangent case: (x₁−ei)²(x₃−ei) = (λei+ν)², x₃ = x([2]P). Just divide both sides by (x₁−ei)². Formally, φi([2]P) = φi(P)² ≡ 1 also follows from the homomorphism property (Step 14) alone (the target is a group of exponent 2), but this explicit formula is the source of "the computability of 2-descent" ── it's thanks to this formula that L = ℚ(√−1, √2) could be computed by hand in Part 2.

? Where the special-value rule comes from

At points with y ≠ 0, (x−e₁)(x−e₂)(x−e₃) = y² ≡ 1, i.e. the product of the square classes of the 3 components is always 1. At T₁ = (e₁, 0) the first component x−e₁ becomes 0 and can't be used, but from the relation product ≡ 1 we can substitute "the product of the other two components": φ₁(T₁) = (e₁−e₂)(e₁−e₃). The rule (e₁−e₃)/(e₁−e₂) in §07 of the main part differs from this only by a factor of (e₁−e₂)², so it's the same square class (confirmed with sympy). You can see the rule is not "an arbitrary convention" but a necessity of the identity.
